## Course Map & The Big Picture
The course trains one skill: problem decomposition, backed by metacognition and a small set of mental models. The structure goes from why to how: the Metaphysics plants the seeds, the 12 Essentials give the 80/20, the Phases of Craftship drill them, and the Value-First tools (guess points, FA²STR, the ideal developer workflow) put them together so you can decompose any design problem. [L1] [L7] [L91]
### The Main Skill: Problem Decomposition + Metacognition [L1]
Pain being solved: the nagging "am I doing this right?" feeling. Developers lack full confidence in their design, architecture, testing and strategy decisions. Requirements keep changing, so they need bespoke approaches, and memorized recipes aren't enough. [L1]
      Definitions
Problem decomposition — "the main skill": breaking a problem into steps you can act on. It applies to front end, back end, desktop, and new or existing code, "because it's all just about problem decomposition." [L1]
Metacognition — awareness of your own thinking. You know how you solve problems and can refine that process. "Great programmers, like philosophers, have metacognition." [L1]
Guess points — the catalog of steps in the problem-decomposition process, and "our problem decomposition technique." [L1] [L7]
The course is organized around mental models, not topics. It touches psychology, philosophy, strategy and subjectivity. [L1] In Khalil's words it is "in essence, a philosophy and metaphysical course… just a course about thinking." [L85]
Design is hard to teach because it is "about you having awareness of how you think and how you learn." [L85]
The course sits one level of abstraction above DDD, testing or FP. It gives you mental models "so that when you're ready to go deeper on those things, you know what to look for." [L91]
“How to use systems thinking, stereotypical architecture, FA²STR and the ideal developer workflow to work consistently and confidently decompose any design problem.” (the main value of the course, stated as a lesson title)[L91]
### What the Course Promises (and What It Demands) [L2]
GoalWrite scalable code in any context: any language, framework or platform, front or back end, greenfield or existing project, new feature. [L2]
ContentThe 80/20 of software design, testing and architecture, compiled into 12 essentials ("the 80/20 of what I've learned studying all of the greats"). [L1] [L2]
Only 4 things matterArchitecture, testing, design and strategy, "whether there's AI, whether there's not AI". The 12 essentials are small, implementable pieces of these four. [L82]
Built onDDD, TDD, BDD, object design, architectural principles and architectural styles. [L2]
Demand"Drill it, drill it, drill it." Skill works like a muscle: stress it consistently. Expect lots of code and lots of design. [L2]
Expect non-linearityDesign feels non-linear. Starting over from scratch is a sign of progress, and it's what makes design feel like an art form. [L2]
      Principles over tools (the woodworker parable)
A woodworker who mastered the plane and drill but never learned which wood to use couldn't build the table when it counted. Tools, techniques and patterns matter, but principles are "thinking tools upon which you can orient yourself." The course aims at phronesis (practical mastery), not just sophia (knowledge). [L13]
Clarity → boredom → creativity: the aim is so much clarity that you're "probably bored". That kind of boredom is good because creativity replaces stress. [L2]
Prerequisites: you're comfortable building front end or back end, you can learn new tech and debug technical snags, and professional experience helps because it gives you more reference experiences. [L1] OOP basics (abstraction, encapsulation, inheritance, polymorphism) are assumed. [L85]
Foundational questions first: What is software? Why do we write it? For whom? What is scalability, and what's stopping you from getting it? Without clarity on these, scalable code isn't reachable. [L1]

The phases describe the natural levels developers pass through. Each one is a different value system for answering "how do I start this project?" [L85] Khalil reverse-engineered them from about 6–7 years of his own study and practice. [L1] [L91]

      The game of value (why Value-First is the top)
Earlier phases treat starting a project as static and linear, like stacking big chunky building blocks. At the deepest level you see software development as a non-linear game of creating and maintaining value, and you win it with the feedback loop (many guesses, guided by principles). That's why "there's no silver bullets." [L85]
Essentials are levelled skills. Think of them like game skills you rank up. At Code-First, horizontal decoupling is about 2/10, and you only work at the "code levels" of the abstraction prism. By Value-First all the physical essentials should be solid. [L82] [L91]
Where the 12 came from: observe & evaluate (survey everything) → eliminate (drop what doesn't matter; the 12 essentials were what remained) → execute. The course is that "straight line" instead of wandering from topic to topic. [L85] "Topics for problems": learn a topic when a problem needs it. [L91]
Right action / quantum moments: what you do at a decision point depends on the phase you're in, meaning what you currently value. More in Mindsets. [L85]
Pattern-First code is deliberately "perfect". You won't hit the ideal at work, but you'll use parts of it. [L91]

### Using the Course to Ship an MVP Fast [L40]
      The workflow is invariant; the effort varies
The steps are the same for an MVP and for a feature on a mature product. Only three things change: time available, effort you're willing to invest, and quality of the guesses you've already made. [L40]
Learn the mental models: the Metaphysics, at least the 12 essentials. They act "like landmines and the opposite of landmines", showing what to avoid and what to move toward. [L40]
Watch the ideal developer workflow (pieced together from GOOS, Continuous Delivery, Clean Architecture, DDD and BDD). [L40] → Workflow
Get clarity on the theoretical guesses: problem, solution (domain boundaries; process modelling for the first one or two user stories), architecture (patterns, tools). Turn their inputs and outputs into a checklist. Use the abstraction prism as the readiness roadmap: "This is also just BDD." [L40]
Pick a testing strategy: at least one acceptance test for customer correctness, plus a configuration of inner-loop tests for the internals. Start with either E2E or a high-value integration test. [L40] → Testing
Build and deploy the walking skeleton. It's messy and takes time, but it establishes the test strategy for the whole product. [L40] Deploy early: the worst case is building for months and finding it can't be deployed. [L82] The first job on a new project is to remove as much uncertainty as possible. [L91]
      Unvalidated MVP = optimize for answers
Priority is getting solid answers to your theoretical guesses (do customers value it?). Heavy testing and polish aren't critical, but still build "on a good foundation of guesses". [L40]
With zero theoretical guesses confirmed, don't do multi-loop TDD at every guess point. Push one guess through fast, then "start playing defense" with physical-level tests. [L85]
Once you have a platform (shared infra, deployment, known services), later features can skip guess points and score faster. [L85]
Top recommended viewing (as of recording): "Acceptance testing a walking skeleton from UI to DB" (Best-Practice-First). Also the Frontend Architecture 2 Q&A (builders, application interface), the theoretical guess points, and the Value-First testing strategy video. [L40] See Frontend and DDD & FeedBook.
### Assignments, Branches & Peer Review [L43] [L41]
Lesson format in the skills phases: a slideshow lesson → a demo → an assignment, or apply the idea to your own project using the provided next steps. [L91] Order after the Code-First assignment: emergent design → stateless testing assignments → Best-Practice-First, starting with acceptance testing a feature. [L82] Katas are in Kata Walkthroughs.
A branch per attempt lets you redo a kata several times, even on the same day, and gives reviewers one URL to comment on. [L43]
Reviewing others' work is mandatory. It mirrors PR review at work and prepares you for the collaborative Bridging the Gaps section. [L41]
Submit your own first, then filter the community by level or theme (e.g., FizzBuzz). [L41]
Pick the oldest unreviewed submission (sort by date). [L41]
Open the PR, glance at Files changed, then go to the first commit and click next through each one, reading it "like a story". You're reviewing the process, not only the final diff. [L41]
Check against the grading checklist, not your personal taste. The checklist is designed so reviewers at any skill level can use it. [L41]
Comment inline, then mark the community post as reviewed so nobody duplicates the work. Optionally redo your own attempt with what you learned. [L41]

## Mindsets (Distilled)
The course's mindset lessons, cut down to the frameworks you can act on: 4 mindsets, 2 obstacles to flow, 2 clarity strategies, 4 motivation techniques, and 2 habits for decisions (thinking backwards, right action). Hype, anecdotes and pep talks are left out. [L3][L4][L5][L6]
### The 4 Key Mindsets & the 90%/10% Framing [L3]
“The value of 90% of my skills just dropped to zero. The leverage for the remaining 10% went up 1000x. I need to recalibrate.” — Kent Beck, on ChatGPT[L3]
The course uses this tweet to frame software essentialism: work out which skills dropped to zero, which gained leverage, and master the ones that matter. [L3]
Software essentialist — Follows the Pareto principle (80% of results from 20% of effort), is value-focused, and goes after perennial skills. A skill that wasn't perennial: being the "Webpack guru". [L3]
1 · Refinement
Ask: is it working and doing what we need? If yes, move on. You can refine later. [L3]
Stop chasing perfect, because returns diminish. Don't get attached to a design: "kill your designs and do it again." An impasse just means it's time to redesign. [L3]
“The road to success is built from bricks of feedback.” [L3]
2 · Clarity
Don't stop at "it depends". Know what it depends on: when to use a tool, when not to, and what its role is. [L3]
Clarify before you code. Think backwards: start from the end state (see below). [L3]
3 · Purpose
“Everything has a purpose”: polymorphism, encapsulation, every tool. Know each one's purpose before you use it. [L3]
Clarity builds confidence, and confidence keeps you going. [L3]
4 · Experience
What counts is how many reference experiences you have and how deep they go, not your senior/junior title. [L3]
If work doesn't give you the chance, practice on your own time (problem decomposition, the 12 essentials). Build up tools and mental models over time. [L3]
Cost of mastery — “Dedication, discipline, and continually returning to the path.” Mastery also means staying in the game. The tedious katas are there to give you reference experiences. [L3][L85]
### The 2 Obstacles to Mastery: Anxiety & Boredom [L4]
Mastery — Skill in a domain where you're “maneuvering in 3D”. You combine ideas creatively, the work feels effortless yet challenging, and you learn fast because you're building on a strong foundation. Example: once you master abstraction design, you can vet tomorrow's libraries and frameworks. [L4]
Flow (extreme focus and presence) is the shortcut to mastery. You can't switch it on. It sits between anxiety and boredom. [L4]
BlockerCauseSymptomsAntidote
AnxietyChallenge exceeds your skill or clarity. You have the wrong mental models, or the puzzle isn't pieced together yet. [L4]Overwhelm, jargon overload (dependency inversion, aggregates, domain events…). Cognitive dissonance: "foot on the gas and the brakes at the same time". You plateau. [L4]Clarity [L4][L89]
BoredomNo challenge. The work feels irrelevant or like a waste of time. [L4]Your mind wanders, you get distracted, and you quit to start something else. [L4]Purpose: tie the work to something that matters to you and set goals that inspire you. [L4]
Cognitive-dissonance detector
If you catch yourself asking “Am I doing this right? Is this best practice? Should I be testing this?” while coding, you're out of flow. Go fix your mental models instead of pushing harder. [L4]
Boredom anti-pattern
Hopping between new libraries, frameworks, jobs and half-started side projects. It gets you no ROI, no growth, and eventually burnout. [L4]
End goal
The coach becomes superfluous. You can then mentor others, and teaching is a higher level of mastery. [L4]
### Clarity Strategies: Mentorship or Essentialism [L5]
Clarity means knowing where you're going and how the pieces fit before you start executing. “It's always going to be the first step.” Without first principles you pattern-match, doubt yourself, defer to others, and relearn the basics with every new tool. [L5]
“If you're experiencing a lot of anxiety when you're doing your work, it's because you don't have enough clarity.”[L89]
Symptoms of low clarity → response
Anxiety
A feeling that it might blow up later, because you have no solid mental model. Treat it as a signal of where your skill is, not as a failure. Be coachable. [L5]
Overwhelm
You're trying to fit DDD, TDD, BDD and RDD into one picture. That's the signal to zoom out. [L5]
Analysis paralysis
Perfectionism: going down every rabbit hole. Aim for a good enough mental model, then act. [L5]
Clarity excludes — Getting clear on what realistically has to happen rules out thousands of other options. Khalil writes a detailed process document before starting, so that implementation “just feels like typing”. Put the what before the how. [L89]
Quantum moments & triple blindness
Quantum moment — A point where one decision drastically changes the outcome. Recognize it and you land on the results timeline; miss it and you land on “the timeline of no results”. [L5][L85]
Examples from the course [L5]:
Writing the test first, or not.
Writing lots of code for a product nobody has validated.
Building a testable architecture from the beginning.
Deriving data and behavior from use cases instead of building up from the DB layer to the API.
Accepting that design is non-linear: like writing, great design comes from redesigning. Don't quit after the first attempt.
Triple blindness is “the greatest manifestation of a lack of clarity” [L5]. It has three layers:
You're not aware that quantum moments exist.
You don't know how to rise to them. For example, you're told to "start writing tests", but how, especially in React?
You don't know what's possible. For example, shipping features in hours, or building about 90% of a React feature without starting React, because it's decoupled from infrastructure and the view is hooked up last.
Strategy 1 — Decide on your mentor
A person (phronimos)
Someone who is where you want to be in design, architecture or testing, and who can still tell you the next step. “Absorb the mentor spirit” (Robert Greene), and aim to surpass your mentor. [L5]
Reality
Khalil's own path: learning from reality itself, using the process of essentialism below. It's slower, and the course exists to shortcut it. [L5]
Strategy 2 — The process of essentialism
Observe & Evaluate — Take in as much information as you can (books, code, blog posts, other developers' views, being corrected) until the bigger puzzle shows. Shorten this step by asking questions in the community. [L5][L85]
Eliminate — Get clear on your purpose or goal, spot the patterns, and keep only the essential forms. Khalil's years of eliminating left the 12 essentials. [L5][L85]
Execute — Commit fully to execution and learn that subset in a “straight line” instead of wandering from topic to topic. [L5][L85]
Use it as a diagnostic — Start from a concrete goal: “What is your current goal?” Many developers “don't even know what they need to know”, so they can't form a question. The course's action item: post one question and help two other developers. Teaching sharpens your own understanding. [L85][L5]
### Endless Motivation: 4 Techniques [L6]
Motivation drops at the first sign of friction, because we default to the path of least resistance. Purpose is the antidote to boredom, and emotional reasons keep you on track better than logical ones. You don't need all four techniques; use them when motivation dips. [L6]
1 · Trail of whys
Ask “why?” again and again until you reach something with an emotional charge. Learn DDD → my codebase is a mess → I want to do a good job → … Spend 5 minutes on paper and keep the real reason front of mind. [L6]
2 · Correct social ties
Peer norms shape behavior. In the study cited, when a friend became obese you were 45% more likely to gain weight; for a friend-of-a-friend it was about 20%. Join a community with high expectations: ask questions, share wins and losses, help others. [L6]
3 · Positive/negative × short/long term
In the short term, discomfort wins (“I don't want to do it”). Write out the long-term vision and the anti-vision (the future you want to avoid) so the long-term difference outweighs short-term comfort. [L6]
4 · Commit to the process
Write a contract or letter to yourself, print it, and keep it where you'll see it every day. “Quality is not an act, but a habit.” [L6]
### Thinking Backwards [L75][L3]
Start at the end: get clear on where you want to be or what something should do, and only then do the work. Khalil calls this “pretty much the whole idea of test-driven development”. [L3]
What do we need to have next? — Name the next result. [L75]
Where do I want to be next? — Name the destination. [L75]
What is the state of the universe I'll be in? — Describe the end state concretely, then let it “carve the pieces moving back” to the present. [L75]
In code
Write the assertion or desired outcome first, then work back to the setup. This is the same move as Arrange-Act-Assert written backwards. Working backwards with TDD “forces you to kind of have faith” (student). [L75][L51]
In life / daily planning
Khalil writes down the desired end state every day. It counters the tendency to dilly-dally without a defined target. [L75]
### Right Action at Quantum Moments [L85]
Quantum moments come up constantly during work. Right action is choosing well at those moments. [L85]
Become aware — Learn to recognize the decision points, starting with “how do I start this project?” [L85]
Know what to do next — What looks "right" depends on the lens you currently see reality through (code-first → best-practices-first → pattern-first → DDD-first → responsibility-first; see Course Map). [L85]
“Right action really, truly, is based on what you currently value.” … “Whatever you place your awareness on … is what you're going to achieve.”[L85]
Heuristic — If you value writing code, you'll get code. If you value creating value, your first thought at the start of a project becomes “we need to be creating value”, and that's where “the game of software design” begins. [L85]

==== 03-metaphysics.html

## Metaphysics: Mental Models & Systems
The first principles of being a developer: what software is, what it is for, who it serves, and how scalability is won or lost. Every later section (12 essentials, guess points, FA²STR, testing, DDD) is built on these models: systems thinking, the value creation system, the 3 roles, the 4 pillars, and the essence → role/goal → purpose → form chain. [L7][L8][L9][L10][L11][L12]
### Why Mental Models Matter [L8]
Metaphysics — “the branch of philosophy that helps us define reality and our understanding of it”. At root it is cause and effect: kick the ball, it moves. In the live sessions Khalil shortens it to “the first principles of reality”. [L8][L94]
Mental model — a simplification of the world (a “map of meaning”), held mostly in the subconscious (~90% of the mind), that lets you interpret reality and choose future action without re-thinking everything. [L8]
Internal representations. Read “dog” or “red” and something pops up automatically. You never consciously chose it, so take ownership of how your subconscious is programmed. [L8]
You are an abstraction designer. Code creates internal representations for you and for every developer who reads it. [L8]
Missing outcomes signal a bad model. A graphic designer who only works in their own style and never sells; a coder who only has fun trying new tech and never gets hired. Something is missing from the model. [L8]
“A mental model is only as good as how close it maps to reality.”[L8]
“You're also an abstraction designer. You are someone who is creating and using mental models.”[L8]
Test for any mental model — (1) Does it produce beneficial outcomes? (2) Does it let you reason from where you are to where you want to be? Good models are based in reality and need first-principles clarity. [L8]
Khalil's two flawed models (and what replaces them)
ModelHow it thinksWhat it produced
API-first“Build this REST endpoint, that REST endpoint, connect front end and back end.”Worked for a while, then turned into expensive messes. [L8]

“Reality is not based on APIs or RESTful endpoints. Reality is much more nuanced and complex than that.”[L8]
Models as levels of thinking: the phases of craftship
The flawed models map to the phases of craftship: code-first → best-practice-first → pattern-first (DDD-first) → responsibility-first → value-first. Each phase is a different lens for seeing the same problem. [L8][L85] Code-first sees “app + DB” blocks. Responsibility-first sees “the fabric of reality itself”. Beyond that, you see reality as value-based, and “the game of software design… actually begins”. [L85] See the full ladder in Course Map.
Quantum moments — moments that matter more than others, where the right action pays off. The classic one is the start of a project: code-first thinking designs from the database; a value-based model uses other techniques that lead to scalable code. What you do in that moment depends on what you currently value (Mindsets → right action). [L8][L85]
“At the start of a project is often one of the most important parts.”[L8]
Reprogram with essential questions — Ask the foundational questions to dig into your subconscious “database” and reorient it: What is software? What is my role? How do I do it well? What is scalable code? The Metaphysics section is organized around them. [L8][L7]
### Systems Thinking: The Most Important Mental Model [L9]
“The world is a patchwork of systems.”[L9]
System — “a model in some environment where there's an input and there's an output.” Purpose of a system: “to achieve a goal”, which means moving from state A to state B. The system between two states can be one system or a composition of many. [L9]
One lens, every scale
ScaleInputModel / processOutput
Function"12:01 - 14:21" (string)military time validator: parse range → validate start → validate end → start before endtrue (boolean) [L9]
Applicationuser actionUI → backend → databasechanged state / response [L9]
Businessideas, demandleadership → marketing → sales → operations (“the thing that does the thing”) → financevalue, revenue [L9][L94]
You, the developerexperiences + intent/goalyour actions and decisionsdesired state. The course keeps refining this model. [L9]
Your processproblem statementFA²STR: each step is its own input → process → outputtested design [L94]
State-transition examples: package not mailed → package mailed; video files acquired → videos edited and exported; unvalidated raw text → validated text. [L9]
Three payoffs
Problem decomposition — breaking any problem into sub-systems and state transitions. Becoming “an excellent problem decomposer” is a main goal of the course. [L9]
Debugging — if you can break a system into parts, you can isolate the part that fails. You spend less time debugging overall. [L9]
Refinement & learning — “You can only really refine if you know what to refine.” [L9]
Anti-pattern: mindless debugging — “What I don't want you to be doing is refreshing the page and just doing mindless stuff.” Reason through the system's parts instead. [L9]
Why it's “the” mental model — The course's two foundational models are the feedback loop and abstraction, “pieced together” by systems thinking: “If you understand those things that is the entire course really.” Tools are also judged as systems: “It's less about the tool. It's more about the inputs, outputs of the system of creating value.” See 12 Essentials I. [L90][L85]
The kata itself is covered in Kata Walkthroughs.
### Essence → Actor/Role/Goal → Purpose → Form (the Mug & the Stone) [L10][L94]
This is the metaphysics behind every abstraction you design. “Perhaps the deepest level of abstraction you need to think theoretically” about why you do anything. DDD and BDD practitioners use it too. [L10]
TermMeaning
EssenceWhat a thing is: all of its properties. Mug: ceramic, has a hole, handle, rounded, has a base, printable. [L94]
ActorA person (e.g. Khalil) who plays many roles: brother, coach, skateboarder, musician; programmer, entrepreneur, writer. [L10][L94]
RoleA part an actor plays. Each role has its own goal. [L10]
GoalWhat the actor in that role wants: the desired state B. [L94]
PurposeWhy the object is used. It comes from role + goal + the chosen how. “It's always up to the actor to decide what the purpose of something is.” [L94]
FormThe properties that are essential for that purpose. [L94]

Hunter → a tool for getting food. [L94]
“Based on who is using it and what they're trying to do, the stone is going to turn into something else.” [L94]
Nuances
Many hows per goal. Energy can come from exercise, coffee or sleep. The mug's purpose only matters once you choose “drink coffee”. [L94]
The actor matters too. A young kid may need the handle for safety, but that still depends on their purpose/goal. [L94]
“There is no one singular purpose of the mug.” [L10]
“The purpose of anything completely depends on the goal of the role.”[L10]
Where features come from — Actor → role → goal → feature/use case → minimal essential properties. This is how you write the minimum amount of code: “We just saw the birth of features.” Run this critical-thinking process every time you design an abstraction. [L94]
“You should be writing no code unless you're very clear on who plays what role and why do they need that.”[L94]
The mug exercise trains you to think about the forms things have and what they combine into. Good OO designers notice when something is pluggable, serializable, and so on. “Printable” already sounds like programming (Serializable, XYZ-able), which is why OOP should start here. [L10][L94] More in Abstraction, OOP & RDD.
What software is (the essence of software)
Purpose of computing: solve problems. Math and automation aren't strictly necessary to solve a problem. [L94]
Essential forms: sequence, selection, iteration. Directions to a store use all three. [L94]
A problem = being in an undesirable state A and wanting a desirable state B. [L94]
Transition = behavior. Event = the fact that a transition happened. Real work is many small steps, and each step is both. [L94]
Data = initial state + events. Behavior acts on state and produces version 2, 3, 4… of the data. [L94]
Namespacing encapsulates data and behavior (an event concept, a transition, a bounded context). Its purpose is understanding: “so really, this is abstraction. This is where abstraction comes from.” Abstraction is the first of the three metaphysical essentials. [L94]
“We have the behavior, which is the transition. And we have the event, which is the fact that a transition happened.”[L94]
Feature / use case (anatomy) — data in → validation rules (how data may come in) + application rules (what may happen to it) → state changes + events out. Its only purpose is to decide whether data may change and to keep things consistent. [L94]
Work with the grain of reality — “The correct architecture is the event-based one.” Reality is event-based, so CRUD-shaped solutions to event-shaped problems don't fit. Event sourcing and CQRS “at the deepest level” strip away everything that isn't the essence of computing. See DDD: FeedBook for state machines and events. [L94]
L94 covers the essence of software. The essence of software development is saved for later: that's the value creation system below. [L94]
### The Value Creation System & the Purpose of Software [L10]
What makes software valuable (user role)
Pain → benefit. Value is created when someone moves from pain to benefit, or remains in benefit, over time. The idea is borrowed from the business world. [L10]
Two mechanisms: time saved (given back) or time enriched. Time is humans' most valuable resource. People spend it working toward their needs (physiological, safety, love, esteem, self-actualization), so solving time-consuming tasks is valued highly. Entertainment and memes are time enriched. “This is why we buy anything.” [L10]
“We are either giving back time or enriching time and we are trying to move people from pain to benefit.”[L10]
Human state management — Multi-step human processes, each with many states, can be offloaded into software, collapsing days into minutes. [L10]
Before — human runs every state
Assemble the package → travel through traffic → post office closes at 4 pm (you work 9–5) → retry another day → package mailed. 3–7 days. [L10]
After — software manages the states
Assemble the package (~10 min) → courier pickup → “package mailed” notification (~2 min to read). Time given back. [L10]
Software artifact of value — a thing that is valuable because it creates human state changes over time (pain → benefit, or remain in benefit). [L10]
“Do not discount the power you have, my friend, as a software developer. It is some serious, magical stuff.”[L10]
The system that produces those artifacts
Value creation system — the developer's work end to end. Theoretical work (high level) → physical work (low level) → a software artifact of value out of the system and in users' hands. [L10] Artifacts move left → right: from “they don't really exist yet and we don't even know if people want this” to “done and in somebody's hands using it”, as fast as possible. [L85]
Rules of the game
Theoretical first, then physical. Go left to right. [L85]
Score, then score faster. Getting one artifact through is “scoring a point in the soccer net”. After that, keep doing it faster “without introducing negative value, which is bugs”. [L85]
Contending elements: domain complexity, team size, and how many theoretical guesses you've gotten right before. [L85]
Every guess can be wrong. Wrong physical guesses become negative value (bugs), and tests are how you check your guesses. Upfront design is a guess too: “The more work we do here, the better our guess is probably going to be down here.” [L94]
Zero validated theoretical guesses? Don't run multi-loop TDD at every guess point. Push one guess through fast, then “start playing defense and adding tests at the physical level.” See Testing. [L85][L94]
Platform strategy: once shared infrastructure exists (deployment, architecture, known services, tested code), you can skip guesses at some guess points and score faster. [L85]
Tools belong to guess points. Big-picture event storming → problem; tactical event storming → solution; impact mapping and user story mapping → theoretical guess points. Processes change, but the value creation system “will never change”. See 12 Essentials III. [L85]
The 12 essentials are the skills that move code through this system: from requests, to deployment to the user, to product metrics, back to leadership deciding what to build next. [L90]
Before any code — “It is not advisable to write code without clarity on at least the who, what, why, and how.” [L10]
There are two more roles, and what makes software hard is that several roles with different goals interact. See the next topic. [L10]
### The 3 Roles & Their Goals: Why Software Development Is So Hard [L11]
Game theory — the study of how and why we make decisions among active agents trying to reach goals. Chess is a two-player game with a single goal (checkmate). Software development is a team sport, but one full of contending elements. [L11]
“Strategy gets introduced when you have multiple roles, multiple goals, and they're all contending to get to their goals.”[L11]
RoleGoal (L11)Goal as restated in the 1-on-1 (L94)
UserGet time back, or have time enriched. [L11]No bugs; a correct solution to the actual problem. [L94]
CustomerIncrease revenue or protect revenue (make money, save money). [L11]Fast builds that cost less. The customer and user can be the same actor. [L94]
DeveloperKeep users and customers in benefit at the same time, and set things up so the key developer use cases stay easy over time. Developers are paid when users and customers stay in benefit. [L11]Build effectively; keep the code maintainable. [L94]
Contending elements
These make winning hard: the status, structure and maintainability of the codebase; the market; the competition; how much time you have; the skill level of the team. [L11]
Assistive elements
These help you win: the 4 pillars (architecture, design, testing, strategy). [L11][L12]
Key developer use cases
Developers work against features, the building blocks of artifacts of value (see vertical slicing in 12 Essentials II). The use cases are to discover & understand (especially in a new codebase), test, extend/change, add, and debug features. [L11]
Why it's hard: constraints shrink the option set — “Make $1,000 by the end of the month” can be done many ways. Add “as a landscaper” and the options narrow. A developer carries three goal sets at once, so the space of acceptable actions is small. You need “extremely effective mental models” that fit all three. [L11]
Scalability — “the word that best describes what it is we're trying to achieve as the apex of all three of those goals.” [L11] Students' concrete goals (easy to change as features are added, many devs on one codebase, easy to navigate, fewer maintainers needed) all reduce to this: “Scalability is when one two three are winning.” [L90]
Negative value (moving from benefit to a bad place) — bugs, defects, regressions; missing requirements; incomplete implementations; features that take longer and longer to build (“This one hits close to home”); codebases that get harder and harder to change. The antidote is mental models “closely aligned with reality”. [L11]
### The 4 Pillars: Architecture, Design, Testing & Strategy [L12]
The four pillars are the assistive elements that make scalability possible. Scalability is achieved by doing all four well and lost through bad design, bad architecture, bad testing or bad strategy. [L12][L90]
Architecture
“Will the structure support the long term goals of these three roles?” [L12]
Covers: architectural styles, patterns, components, tools, how to break up and organize code, frameworks and libraries, project structure, monolith vs split, shared folders. [L12]
Classic violation: not starting a project with testing in mind. For example, building frontend components on top of components and then testing only through them, which makes useful tests hard to write. [L12] See Architecture.
Design
How easily can other developers discover and understand how to perform their key developer use cases against the codebase? [L12]
Tools: folder structure, signs, symbols, names, consistency, design patterns. “Design patterns themselves are mental models”: observer/Redux is an object in the middle that emits events to subscribers. [L12]
Symptom: you open a project (or your own old code) and can't tell what it does. Example: packaging by infrastructure (routes/, controllers/) says nothing about what the code does. [L12][L90]
Testing
Can I define what the system does (ahead of time) and be aware whether it still does it successfully? [L12]
Covers: test strategies, types of tests, TDD schools (classic, inside-out, mockist, outside-in), and CI/CD that runs tests in a pipeline before production. [L12]
Main reason people struggle to write tests: coupling, “the enemy of testing”. [L12][L90] See Testing.
Strategy
How can we make the most effective use of the contending and assistive elements we currently have? [L12]
Elements: time, money, current codebase quality, the team, the market, the competition. Know techniques and when to use them: how much architecture, design and testing is right, which shortcuts to take now, where to invest, and what to do quick and dirty. [L12]
Design deep-dive: Don Norman's two gulfs
From The Design of Everyday Things: between a goal and knowing whether you reached it are two gaps. Use them to judge developer experience. [L12]
Practice — Build awareness of how quickly and smoothly you (and others) can discover how to perform each developer use case. Think from a developer-experience point of view. [L12]
Why you can't learn one pillar in isolation
Every topic sits in a mesh of the four pillars. Clean code is mostly design, partly architecture, somewhat testing. Stubbing/mocking is mostly testing, a bit of design, some architecture, and strategy (when to use them). The same goes for DDD, BDD, encapsulation, polymorphism, composition over inheritance, programming against an interface, and testing against a production-like environment. [L12]
“It is a fabric of topics that are related to each other.” Proper end-to-end testing is a graph of many essential subtopics. Decoupling with an event-based backend needs vertical slicing, temporal decoupling, and messaging (a massive topic). “It's a complexity explosion.” Be easy on yourself. [L12]
Essentialism: find the few things that produce most of the results and refine them. The claim relayed about top developers is that they're more productive “not by a factor of 10x or 100x or even 1000x, by 10,000x.” [L12]
Raise the level of thinking. “Information is consciousness. Consciousness is information.” Getting closer to first principles means solving problems at a deeper level instead of at “fractured points”. [L12]
“Mastery takes time. It is an untangling process.”[L12]
The 12 essentials detangle the pillars — They're broader than techniques and patterns but low enough that you know what to do next. “Perhaps even more helpfully, they help us understand what it is we should not do.” Use them like RPG stats that start low and level up. They also work as self-coaching questions: Am I respecting vertical slicing? Do I have clarity over the subject under test? Am I decoupling concerns horizontally? See 12 Essentials. [L12]

### Abstraction: Taming Complexity [L14]
"Thought works by abstraction." The Chinese character for "man" started as a head-and-legs figure. It cuts out detail and leaves a simple image you can grasp. A washing machine's start cycle button and a TV remote's power button are public interfaces. The low-level steps behind them stay hidden and only the builders need to know them. [L14]
“It's a way to tame complexity, which is the default state of the universe.” … “Never forget that you yourself are abstraction designer.”[L14]
Definitions of abstraction
The root principle of design, where design means discoverability + understanding (Norman's two gulfs) for the key developer use cases: discover, understand, add, change, debug, test, plan features. [L14] "It's what from how." [L82]
Separating the theoretical from the physical, visualized by the abstraction prism. [L14]
Your highest-level understanding of the problem (getting from here to there) plus the concrete, discrete steps to get there, written as past-tense events, states or steps. Smaller steps sit behind them, "abstracted away from us". [L91]
Many names for the same split: essential vs accidental complexity, upfront vs emergent design, theoretical vs physical, what vs how, declarative vs imperative [L14]. Also high vs low level, contract vs concretion, abstract vs real, type vs implementation [L93]. It's hard to grasp because it is everywhere; it is "the fabric of how we achieve goals". The developer's job is to be a problem decomposer who uses abstraction to break problems down. [L93] The "abstract vs real" axis is developed as levels of realness. [L91]
Use case: add a feature [L14]
Package by infrastructure (folders named after technical parts) tells you nothing about where a feature goes. Screaming architecture / package by feature (module, domain) lines the folders up with goals, so it's obvious where to add one. See Architecture.
Use case: change a feature [L14]
Deeply nested technical code is hard to trace. A Gherkin feature file (feature + scenarios) that informs an executable specification reads like a table of contents. You find the scenario to change, or add an edge case.
### The Abstraction Prism: Theoretical Above, Physical Below [L14] [L93]
The prism starts from the theoretical questions before any code (Who are we helping? What will we help them achieve? What will they be enabled to do?) and narrows down to code. Khalil returns to it whenever he decides how to structure or organize code. [L14] He also built his course "topic map" on it (goals → capabilities → features → acceptance criteria → deeper). [L91]
Chapters vs sub-chapters. High-level events are the chapters (1 → 2 → … → end), "the truth of the story". Code is the sub-chapters along the path. Those events form a state machine: dirty clothes → … → washed clothes, where one event (start cycle) is a slice holding many low-level steps. [L14]
Directionality. "We always need to start from the top and go downwards." Coding from the bottom up toward the vision "always results in a mess". Khalil calls this "the major big learning". [L93]
Top-down sequence: who (roles) → why (goals) → what (features) → stories → acceptance criteria → concrete examples → validated → then code. [L93]
The highest theoretical abstraction in code is the acceptance test / feature file, with stories, acceptance criteria and a table of concrete examples. If someone can read it and know exactly what the software should do, you have the top-level abstraction. [L93]
Code-first developers live only in the code levels of the prism. They can connect a frontend to a backend but can't connect an endpoint to acceptance criteria, goals or roles. [L91]
Ideal codebase entry path [L93]
README says "go to the tests" → tests folder broken down by capability → one feature file per feature → stories → scenarios with concrete examples → then into the code.
Physical layers look alike everywhere [L14] [L90]
React components and Express request handlers both deal with the outside world. Both are interfacers and belong to the infrastructure layer. The same stereotypical layers appear on frontend, backend and desktop: "We're doing the exact same things on the front end on the back end." Knowing this stereotypical architecture brings calm about where things belong. See Physical essentials and Architecture.
### Working Top-Down: Start From the End, Think Backwards [L14] [L93]
Start from the very end: clarify the end goal. In practice, clarify your theoretical guesses upfront and embed them in the codebase as high-level tests that describe features and scenarios with concrete examples. [L14]
Think backwards: this narrows the ways you could reach the goal. [L14] (Deep dive: [L75].)
Write the minimum code that reaches the goal. Less code means less to maintain, which is why TDD matters. [L14]
Theoretical first, then physical. In the physical, write tests and code together and move deeper toward the core. [L14]
Know the purpose (the theoretical side) of every abstraction before you implement it. [L14]
Role → goal → features → concrete examples → acceptance tests is the 1-2-3 chain that drives all work. "Never start a project without these four words clearly understood": who, why, what, how. [L82] Example: DDD Forum had weak motivation while it was "just build these endpoints". Reframed, it serves a conference organizer whose goal is to "obtain an audience of devs curious about DDD to sell tickets to". Its features become register, buy tickets, view ticket advertisement, ask questions about DDD. [L82] [L93]
Self-check while coding [L93]
"What is the actual value of me doing this thing… how can I connect this to a role's goal?" If you can't answer, you're thinking forwards, which means reacting without clarity on why.
Anti-patterns
Perfectionism / rabbit-holing: aiming for chapter 1 → 8 but stuck in 1.1 → 1.1.1 → 1.1.2… In code, the goal is a frontend app, yet you're deep in Webpack's nuances and then its plugins. Start from the end goal and stop once the problem is solved. [L14] [L93]
Code-first development: throwing tools and technologies at problems. [L14] Thinking in endpoints instead of features, and skipping concrete examples to write code directly. [L82]
API-first / code-level-first design: "extremely common". Khalil recommends BDD in Action. [L93]
Example A: code-first vs abstraction-first
Before: HOW first. The WHAT is never written down
After: WHAT as an executable spec, then the minimum HOW
Example B: a test as a "flag post in the future"
“Writing tests is just setting a flag post in the future to explain what we are about to realize.”[L93]
Project into the future, then bridge the gap with code. The test declares the value you are about to deliver and tells you when you're done. [L93] The concrete example below is the DDD Forum "buy tickets" scenario. Khalil narrowed "billed + emailed" down to "an order placed" because the rest should be temporally decoupled. [L82]
The protocol driver and the four-layer acceptance-test stack are covered in Testing. [L82]
### The Feedback Loop: Everything Is a Guess [L15]
Take a frontend-only registration feature. It already contains many guesses: present the form, maintain form state, validate, marshal data, make the request, show a spinner (that actually works), present errors, and navigate to onboarding on success. Each one is an acceptance criterion you need confidence in. [L15]
“Everything that we do in software development is one big guess.” … “We're going to make a lot more mistakes than we will successes.”[L15]
Definition: feed forward, get feedback, learn, and make the next attempt faster and better. Refine one system before moving to the next. [L91]
Never go too far without knowing whether the abstractions you just made work. That's why testing comes right after code-first. [L82]
The first guess is theoretical: "does anyone even care?" (e.g. an anime debate app). You validate it with value identification. [L93] A student summary Khalil endorsed: "Clarity means less guesses." [L93]
No known alternative: you can't reach a goal without splitting it into high and low levels (abstraction) and trying, failing, learning and retrying (feedback loop). [L93]
It takes the pressure off. The course itself was built by abstraction plus feedback loop. "You're not going to get whatever it is you're trying to do right the first time around… but do it again." This connects to the mindsets kill your designs and design it twice; see Mindsets. [L90]
The 3 problems with guesses [L15]
1. Not knowing what guesses you're making
This is typical of code-first developers who don't know the abstraction prism or abstraction layers. Layers are buckets for your abstractions and for implementing guesses. Fix: abstraction prism and guess points.
2. Not knowing how to validate a guess
Many developers only black-box test through E2E tests. Fix: more targeted tests via subject/system verification and Testing.
3. Loops that aren't tight enough
Write code, save, wait to compile, eyeball it. Answers arrive late. Manual testing doesn't build confidence, and its cost grows with the code and the team.
Tests are the maintainable fix [L15]
Every feature is a guess, and the number of guesses only grows. The only maintainable way to keep re-validating all of them is to cover them with tests. Also: clarify before building, and check with users that you built the right thing.
### Guess Points: A Map of Where the Guesses Live [L15]
The guess points are the value creation system seen through systems thinking. They apply abstraction and the feedback loop to decompose problems. Each guess point is a small input → output system with its own questions, mostly answered with tests. The output of one is the input of the next. "It's just systems thinking and it's just inputs and outputs." [L15]
Theoretical guess points: problem (vision, role, goal, capabilities; its output is the input to the solution), then solution, then architecture. Each contains smaller systems that bring the abstraction closer to code. [L15]
Physical guess points (past architecture): e.g. incoming adapter, stateful domain modelling and others. Each has its own questions, answered mostly with tests. They come from heuristics, mostly the stereotypical architecture (responsibility-first phase). [L15]
Frontend too? Yes. Frontend patterns come in pattern-first, then responsibility-first, then back to guess points in value-first. [L15]
More guess points? Possibly. It's Khalil's decomposition layout. A code review process, for example, is another system with its own inputs, outputs, processes and environment. [L15]
When to use: not right away. You first climb the phases of craftship. Guess points are the main decomposition approach in value-first, where you "just do the things that have the most value at the right time". [L15] [L82]
Walked example: slot-booking system notifying ~7 services [L93]
Problem clarity: value identification / impact mapping (who, what, impact, capabilities).
Solution clarity: domain discovery. Big-picture event storming first, then process-model one story end to end.
Problem-decomposition guess point: the experience, screens, data in and out, speed needs (maybe WebSockets).
Architecture guess point: tools, tests, CI tests, and whether to go event-based from day one.
Walking skeleton: then move into the physical.
Psychic entropy [L93]
Without a consistent process, decomposing a problem feels overwhelming. Knowing which guess point you're at tells you whether you have enough clarity to move forward. The full process is in FA²STR & the ideal developer workflow.
### Debugging by Zooming Out [L82]
If you keep changing something and it keeps failing, stop. "You're just so too deep into the how." Example: Khalil and a collaborator editing Dockerfile lines one at a time, with a 30-second feedback cycle on each change. [L82]
Stop the change-fail loop.
Zoom out and draw it upfront.
Clarify what needs to happen and which responsibilities are involved.
Descend one level and act again.
“When I'm coding, I actually spend very, very little time debugging because I always make sure I start with the question, do I know what I'm doing?”[L82]

### Vertical Slicing [L16] [L93]
Definition
Decomposing work into manageable chunks, meaning vertical slices of functionality. You chunk modules, features and operations into slices so that features are first-class citizens in the code structure and in how you approach the work. [L16]
Feature
Valuable to the customer. Described by a user story that takes someone through an experience from A to B, involving many API calls. Example: “register and sign up for a premium package on a membership website.” [L16]
Operation
A lower-level slice: a command (changes the system, e.g. createUser) or a query (reads it, e.g. getProfileDetails). In practice an API call, a thin input→output slice. A sequence of operations builds a feature. [L16] [L93]
Module / capability
The thick slice: a high-level module, bounded context or capability (profile management, friending, settings). Features/workflows sit between modules and operations. Cohesion decides how thick to cut. [L93]
Why
Design = discoverability and understanding. It must be easy to find what you need. [L16]
It matches reality: developers work against features. The key developer use cases are to discover, understand, add, change, remove, test and debug features. [L16]
Your mental model shows up in the code. Other developers read it back out. Thinking in “tiny scripts” produces tiny scripts. [L16]
The most valuable thing to test is the feature, not createUser/editUser in isolation. Features come out of the theoretical guess points, and acceptance tests are written at the physical ones. [L16]
How
Start from features — derived from customer needs and user stories, not from endpoints. [L16] [L82]
Cut modules by capability — “feel into” how thick to cut, judged by discoverability and understanding. Vaughn Vernon’s domains → bounded contexts → sub-bounded contexts is an optional deeper cut. [L93]
Make features visible in folders and tests — a feature can be a folder, a file, or just a feature file in tests. Ideal entry path: README says “go to the tests” → tests by capability → a feature file per feature → stories → scenarios with concrete examples → code. [L93]
Navigate like a table of contents — source → modules → domain → feature → operation → the component misbehaving. This works when combined with horizontal layers (the grid below). [L18]
Self-check — Can you see the features in your code base? Could someone else find them? How hard would it be? [L16]
Before — package by infrastructure: one feature is smeared across five folders
After — package by feature: the folder tree reads like the product
Anti-patterns
Packaging by infrastructure: folders by technical type hurt design, discoverability and understanding. [L16] Khalil: “I hate packaged by infrastructure.” [L88]
Transaction-script thinking: treating work as “just adding code” to tiny scripts. [L16]
Starting from React components: “not the worst thing,” but it doesn’t match the key use case of working against features. [L16]
Thinking in endpoints instead of features, typical of code-first. [L82]
Testing operations as if they were the most valuable unit. [L16]
When interfaces make navigation painful
Khalil’s three-part answer: (1) slice features into feature folders, (2) use cohesive folder structure and naming (a name also says what something is not), (3) follow the layered-architecture rules so you know what to expect, since use cases always reference interfaces. [L84]
“If you can't see your features from the folder structure alone there's a good chance maintainability is under pressure.”[L16] [L93]
“It is the internal representations that we have in our minds, the mental models that we're working off of, that's what is going to show up in our code.”[L16]
Roadmap: the main vertical-slicing work happens in Best Practice First (feature slicing). Pattern First adds read/write path separation and read/write models. [L16] Deeper structure in Architecture & Code Structure; features come from the theoretical essentials.
### Horizontal Decoupling [L17] [L84] [L88]
Definition: two parts
Awareness: know the well-known layers of abstraction, their directionality and stereotypes, so every line of code goes into the right abstraction instead of “no abstractions and just writing code.”
Actual decoupling: strategically decouple those layers with dependency inversion (contracts), so you can isolate and put a boundary around what you want to verify.
It breaks down what happens inside an operation (a vertical slice) into categories of work, called concerns. [L17] [L93]
Why
Locality: if the DB connection breaks, DB code lives in one known place. Less duplication: duplication appears when nobody knows where things belong, and the code “turns into mush.” [L17]
Testing options. Application and domain hold the edge cases, business rules, validation and policies, which is the most valuable code to test. Without decoupling you can only reach it by starting the whole app end to end, which means slow tests and slow pipelines. [L17]
Black-box testing only gives result verification. Edge cases such as “deactivate without delete, then rejoin → welcome back” become nearly impossible to cover through the black box. [L84]
Fast CI. Decoupling lets fast tests run in CI instead of E2E-only suites. [L93]
Two problems of the naive backend, and their fixes [L88]
E2E tests don’t scale. Every feature test goes through the whole app to the DB, and setting up DB state (fixtures) each time is slow and complex. Fix: dependency inversion. Describe persistence (and external services like a PaymentService) as a contract. Core code “uses / relies on” it; the real DB, Stripe or a fake in-memory version “is a / plays the role of” it. “It's always way faster and easier to manipulate something in memory rather than in disk.”
Controller, application logic and business logic are fused, so even with a fake DB you still go through Express. Fix: an application interface. All use cases are composed into one pure object; the app implements it and the web server uses it (see Composition).
A third problem is lack of encapsulation: pulling validation into a random function in another file keeps data and behavior apart. [L88]
Before — fat route: HTTP, rule and SQL in one place, testable only end to end
This is the “anemic domain model” shape. There’s no place where the rules live, and no horizontal decoupling. [L84] Almost everybody starts here, and it “looks kind of elegant.” [L88]
After — slim controller → use case → repository contract; fakes make the core testable
Keep the fake honest with a shared contract test [L81].
How: rules
Know the layers: infrastructure → adapter → application → domain. MVC, Clean, Onion, Ports & Adapters/Hexagonal are all related (“it is all related”). They are one stereotypical architecture you “can't unsee” once you know it. [L17] [L88]
Stereotype each abstraction. A React component presents, accepts input and passes it off (interfacing), so it belongs to infrastructure and never touches a database. [L17]
Classify code by what it touches, not where it comes from. An npm library that just returns a string is core. Express is infrastructure because it opens a port. [L84]
Use cases depend on interfaces (roles), and concretions (actors) are injected via the constructor. “The repository is the adapter… it is a role to be played.” [L84] [L88]
Start with an interface, and move to an abstract class only after the rule of three shows a real need to share implementation. [L84]
Keep controllers slim: pull data out of the request and pass it to a service layer. The evolution is view → controller → DB, then carve out a service layer (four-tier), then a pure application + domain core behind contracts. [L82]
Know the rules well enough to break them knowingly, and to marshal “mush” back into testable code later. [L17]
Quiz from the live session
Where does await this.repo.save(post) belong? Answer: in the application service / use case (application layer, high-level persistence logic), not in a controller. [L93]
Decoupling from time: the clock contract
new Date() and Math.random() are infrastructure because they touch the system clock and random generator. The domain model must be pure: no system time, no Math.random, no setTimeout. Otherwise tests have to wait (e.g. 30 s for a traffic light). Put a clock contract in between. Khalil notes that passing the date value straight into the constructor is “not great.” [L84] [L88]
Before — hidden dependency on the system clock
After — time is a role; tests control and travel it
Anti-patterns
Responsibilities living in the wrong place, which leads to duplication, confusion and “mush.” [L17]
All logic in the Express controller (anemic domain model): only black-box testable. [L84]
Injecting a concrete DB/ORM into the use case, which makes it infra-dependent and slow to test. [L84]
A React component that knows about a database. [L17]
this.repo.save(...) in a controller. [L93]
Writing code “until the thing works” with no thought to where things belong. “You haven't been bit yet.” [L93]
“What we want here is some order, some structure and a clear place for abstractions to go.”[L17]
“Ultimately, what we need is more testing options… The skill that is going to give you more testing options is the skill of dependency inversion.”[L17]
“It's not about where it comes from it's about what it touches.”[L84]
Roadmap: MVC in Code First → layered architecture plus 4-tier non-flaky E2E tests in Best Practice First → core vs infrastructure and ports & adapters in Pattern First → stereotypical architecture in Responsibility First. [L17] More in Architecture and Abstraction & RDD.
### Temporal Decoupling [L18] [L93]
Definition
Decoupling functionality with events, instead of slotting new operations into existing ones. When a vertical slice completes, it publishes an event (e.g. OrderPlaced). Event handlers listen and invoke new vertical slices, sometimes in entirely different systems. [L18]
Coupling is “the tightness or the interconnectedness of modules or lines of code.” [L18]
Why
Requirements are temporal. They arrive as “after this do that,” “before this happens,” “only when this happens, if this is already true,” or “if in production/dev.” These are policies. [L18] “The nature of reality is… temporal… It's event based.” [L93]
Appending breaks things. Steps block each other. If the notification service fails, the important steps after it never run and the system is left undefined. To verify one follow-up you must run everything. [L18]
Speed. If Amazon billed, emailed, updated inventory, created fulfillment and logged analytics inside “place order,” requests would be too slow, and “probably no one would be using Amazon.” [L18]
All-or-nothing. Never leave “billed the customer but inventory not updated.” Khalil did exactly that with a makePurchase transaction script on the solidbook site, and serverless cold starts cut requests midway. [L88]
Hard to retrofit. Temporal coupling is “only solvable… from the very start,” so design event-based architecture from day one. [L93]
Before — one request awaits every follow-up, and one failure strands the rest
After — the slice ends at “order placed”; handlers start new slices
Added by Copilot An in-process bus loses events on a crash. Production backends need persistence and exactly-once/outbox-style delivery, which Khalil lists as backend concerns of the coordinator [L93].
How
Hear the time words — treat “after / before / when / only if” as signals for events and policies. [L18]
End the slice at its own state change — the Gherkin “then” became “an order should have been placed” rather than “he is billed / emailed,” because that work must be temporally decoupled. [L82]
Publish, then react — handlers call follow-up slices. Start simply with a coordinator (the object stereotype whose one job is to take in events and let other parts know; basically event listeners), and make it robust later. [L93]
Keep flow unidirectional — event → bus → command → command handler, cycling one way, as Redux does. [L93]
Model success, then failure — for each decoupled step, ask what happens if it fails and design compensating events (billing fails → email, wait, cancel after 3 days, release inventory). [L18] [L88]
Use it to find aggregate boundaries — if you can’t find the boundary, “you're probably trying to do too much in one command.” [L88]
Frontend vs backend
Front ends likely already have this: every front-end architecture needs reactivity, and Redux does it. The backend holds more high-level policy, so decoupling operations and thinking through success/failure paths matters more there. [L18]
Anti-patterns
Continually appending functionality after a core action in the same operation. Khalil’s own “after job posted” code was “a terrible way to add functionality that comes afterwards.” [L18]
Sequential blocking calls where one failure prevents other important work. [L18] [L93]
A mail service as a direct dependency of CreateUser. Khalil corrects his own diagram for this. [L20]
Terminology note
The course describes idempotency as “anytime we do anything in our system, it's going to keep the system in a consistent state” [L18], and uses it for “either this whole thing works or none of it works” [L88]. Added by Copilot Standard usage: idempotent means repeating an operation has the same effect as doing it once, which is vital for retried handlers. All-or-nothing is atomicity.
“The reality is that requirements are temporal.”[L18]
“When you do place order, what's happening is Amazon is dispatching an event.”[L18]
Khalil’s own caveat: “This topic is even one that I am not particularly an expert in. I just know a lot from the experts.” [L18] Roadmap: unit of work and event-based backend architecture in Pattern First; custom builds in Responsibility First. [L18] Events also drive event storming and process modelling.
### Composition [L19] [L93]
Definition
Consciously, not unconsciously, creating a network of collaborating abstractions. You pass in each part’s required dependencies, and their dependencies, like Russian dolls. Done well, the app is configurable from one location and dynamic runtime behavior is easy. It is “more than just a passing design principle” like “prefer composition over inheritance.” [L19] [L93]
Truth 1: software is made of many parts
Code-first developers wire parts unconsciously: copy from docs, Stack Overflow or ChatGPT, then plug things together until it works. The result is cyclical dependencies and code that is hard to understand, maintain and, most critically, test. [L19]
Truth 2: software runs in many environments
Dev, test, CI, staging, production. Some things toggle per environment (e.g. Redux dev tools on in dev). Handling this needs polymorphism, “the ability for your code to take on different shapes.” [L19]
How
Decouple first — composition depends on horizontal decoupling. Without concerns in buckets there’s “nothing to swap”; it’s “jiggly puff type code.” [L93]
Respect directionality — outer-layer concerns rely on inner-layer ones. [L19]
Trace the graph — follow every dependency all the way up to the web server. “It forces you to really have a tight awareness of how you're linking up your code base.” [L19]
Encapsulate wiring in a composition root — one object does all linking and holds the environment checks. Stop importing things “willy nilly” in Express controllers, which hides the object collaborations: “Apps are all made from objects that talk to each other.” [L19] [L82]
Grow deliberately — start with a composition root (poor man’s DI). Switch to a DI container only when it gets messy, per Mark Seemann’s effort-vs-messiness graph in Dependency Injection. [L93]
Before — environment checks and dependencies grabbed inline
After — dependencies passed in; environment decided once
Where it pays off
Testing: swap dependencies at the application guess point (and the system and incoming-adapter guess points). You can test an entire front-end feature without starting React or setting up Redux. [L19]
Application interface: clumping all use cases into “one big object” is an instance of composition. Incoming-adapter tests then only check that endpoints call the correct use cases with the correct parameters, without running them. Without it, as a student put it, “you end up building your app within your framework.” [L88]
Deployment pipelines: code must be composable up front to run in many environments. “We 1000% will have to use it.” [L19]
Reproducible dev infra: encapsulate provisioning in the code base (Docker instead of a local DB). [L82] [L81]
Plugin architectures: VS Code extensions and themes, new weapons in a game, audio nodes in a DAW. Aim for an app that is “polymorphic in general.” [L19] [L93]
Code review from the live session: connections
A student built each module’s repository per request (async UserRepository.build()) to avoid long-lived DB connections. The consensus: per-request connections cost handshakes and suffer serverless cold starts, and callers might forget to await the async build. Instead, create a DbConnection abstraction once at bootstrap and inject it into thin repositories through the composition root. [L93]
Anti-patterns
Copy-paste-plug-until-it-works wiring; treating design as only folder structure. [L19]
Cyclical dependencies from unconscious linking. [L19]
Environment checks littered through the code base. [L19] [L93]
Front-end code that can only be tested E2E because abstractions aren’t known or placed. [L19]
“The way in which we link together our dependencies is incredibly important.”[L19]
“It's about creating dynamic behavior and that we need to link things together almost like a Russian doll.”[L19]
Roadmap: mainly Best Practice First (refactoring code-first work with composition), used heavily in Pattern First; DI containers in Pattern/Responsibility First. [L19] [L93]
### Subject/System Verification [L20] [L84]
Definition
Using tests to place a boundary around the thing you want to verify, so you can tell whether it helps achieve a goal. It treats the application as nested input/output systems, decomposed by vertical slices and horizontal layers, so you can isolate specific parts. [L20] Khalil prefers the term subject. [L88]
Why
“The reason why we write tests is because everything we're doing is a guess.” This is the feedback loop made physical. [L20]
Developers struggle with testing because it’s treated as an afterthought instead of something foundational. Learning it “means learning 18 other things at the same time.” [L20] [L93]
If you can’t draw the boundary you need, that tells you to apply more horizontal decoupling, vertical slicing or composition. “It all builds on each other.” [L93]
Three types of verification
1 · Result
Inputs → outputs; the environment is unchanged. Simplest, and the only kind possible through a black-box E2E test. [L20] [L84]
2 · State
Act on a loaded model with no direct output, then reach into its state. Found by programming by wishful thinking: write the assertion with the API you wish existed. [L20] [L84]
3 · Communication
Verify the subject talks properly to its dependencies: indirect inputs (repo fetch) and indirect outputs (repo save). Hardest, but it “is going to lead to the tests that are the most valuable.” [L20] [L84]
Result and state verification are often combined in the same test, which is “completely fine.” [L20] Full technique coverage (spies, builders, AAA backwards) is in Testing [L53].
How to choose the boundary
Name the guess — the test type depends on the guess you are validating. Example: do API calls route to the right use cases and do contracts still hold? That needs a boundary from the outside world to the use-case entry. [L93]
Pick the layer — for API stability across mobile, web and desktop clients, test at the incoming adapter. For “a job posting cannot be edited after 48 hours live,” test in the domain layer. [L20]
Widen for side effects — to verify a welcome email after createUser (temporally decoupled), the subject must include the event infrastructure for UserCreated, plus a spy on the mail service. [L20]
Prioritise features — the most valuable thing to test is what’s most valuable to the customer. [L20]
Make fast tests valuable — “We want the tests that run really fast to be tests that are also incredibly valuable”: high-value unit tests in the pipeline’s fast stage. [L20]
“We're only testing our mocks” → contract tests
That feeling means the fake and prod implementations lack test parity. Run one integration spec across all implementations of the interface: “You're testing the contract, not the implementation.” Use library mocks only for trivial stubs (a logger); for stateful dependencies, use fakes covered by contract tests. [L81]
Three test categories [L82]
Acceptance: does this do what the customer wants?
Unit: does this do what I, as a developer, think it should? Domain and application logic; about 80% of the work.
Infrastructure: does this infrastructure do what I want (start/stop DB, connect, start/stop web server)?
Anti-patterns
Testing as an afterthought. [L20]
Relying only on E2E/black-box tests for rule-heavy features. [L84]
Getting lost in mock vs stub vs fake taxonomy; just say “test double.” [L84]
A fake repo and a prod repo tested separately with no shared contract. [L81]
“It's us placing a boundary around the thing we want to verify to be able to tell if it's going to help us achieve a goal.”[L20]
Roadmap: manual testing in Code First → test infrastructure and acceptance tests in Best Practice First → patterns for the most valuable tests in Pattern First → outside-in, test-driven work with FA²STR in Value First, “perhaps one of the most valuable things I can teach you in this course.” [L20]
### Walking Skeleton [L24] [L81]
Definition
“Build the smallest slice of useful functionality and deploy it as quickly as possible,” verified by an automated end-to-end test in the most production-like environment possible. [L24] “It's exactly the same thing as a tracer bullet” (The Pragmatic Programmer): the most bare-bones valuable thing, shipped in front of someone. [L81] It is “a minimal slice of functionality through all of the main architectural components.” [L82]
Scenario 1: components firstScenario 2: skeleton first
What you do~2 weeks of React components and core logic (maybe tested), no DB or web server hooked up; deploy at the endDeploy a minimum slice across everything first. It feels “like hell,” snag after snag
OutcomeUnknown deploy time; infra surprises land last. Khalil’s own version: all the fun pure code first, then “all these weird CORS errors”After a weekend or 1.5 weeks: “something deployed, even if it's a little bit crappy”
VerdictScenario 2: “We don't actually know how long it's going to take for us to get something deployed.” [L24] [L81]
How
Get very clear on the theoretical — the problem and the solution. [L24]
Get clear on architectural components — patterns and how they play together. [L24]
Draw a broad-stroke architecture diagram. [L24] [L93]
Build it and test it end to end — functionality that cuts across all main components, UI → DB. [L24] [L93]
Figure out how to deploy it — with a minimal pipeline, because “working software” means software void of bugs, so even the skeleton needs a guard phase. [L24] [L81]
Decisions the skeleton forces early
Monolith vs monorepo, source control, CI, deployment pipeline, test infrastructure, scripting. [L24] [L93]
A reproducible setup so others can pull, run and test; Docker to encapsulate infrastructure. [L24] [L81]
Developer ergonomics (folder structure, README): “things are admittedly never going to be easier” than at the start. [L24]
Where it sits
Guess points: architecture guess point → (physical) E2E test across everything → deployment & delivery → execution guess point. The question at the end: “Can users even use this thing? Will this even work? Does it deploy?” After users use it, check KPIs, which loop back into what to build next. [L24] [L81]
After the skeleton you’ve “made a pretty big mess.” The rest of Best Practice First improves the design so Pattern First work is effective. [L81] Next come proper scripts, clean runs across environments, working composition, a confirmed test strategy, and tests embedded in the pipeline. [L25]
Existing messy projects: kick off a new feature with a skeleton to pull the codebase toward structure. [L24] For existing features, pin behavior with characterization tests first; don’t rewrite from scratch. [L81]
It is architectural and strategic at once: it sets up design and the testing foundation together. [L82] It is step one of the ideal developer workflow’s physical side.
Anti-patterns
Building components for weeks or months without DB/web server, deploying last. [L24] [L82]
Assuming you can predict the snags of the physical realm: version conflicts, outdated tools, containerization issues. [L24]
Working in front-end or back-end silos without an end-to-end slice. [L81]
“We want to get the most bare bones version of something that is valuable to somebody out shipped in front of them.”[L81]
“Make sure that we can actually score this thing through the goalpost.”[L82]
### Deployment & Delivery [L25] [L93]
Definition
Make sure the tests you write actually gate what ships, so you never ship negative value: “bugs, defects, anything that is going to take the users out of benefit.” The tool is a deployment pipeline, a constraint (from Don Norman’s The Design of Everyday Things, “something that limits you from all the possible sets of things you can do”) that enforces a single path to production and a minimum quality standard. [L25] The ideas come from the Continuous Delivery / DevOps movements: “I'm not the brainiac that invented this.” [L93]
The phases
Commit phase — lint (code looks standard), build, run tests. Ideally these are high-value unit tests, which test features purely and fast, not just front-end component tests. Package the artifact. Passing unit tests should give ~80–90% confidence, which is only possible with a pure, decoupled core. [L25] [L81] [L82]
Release candidate — a deployable artifact (zip, Docker image…), the version that becomes production if later stages pass. [L82]
Acceptance phase — set up infrastructure mirroring production (staging) and run E2E tests as acceptance tests. They test the same things as high-value unit tests, but through the whole system, as a user would. Infrastructure tests can run before or after: “doesn't really matter.” [L25] [L82]
Deploy — manual or automated. [L25]
Rules
One path to production: make it “virtually impossible for someone to ship negative value.” [L25]
Start minimal, then add phases (security, performance). Pipeline speed can keep improving; some people’s whole job is that “runway before code gets out to users.” [L25] [L93]
Fast things pre-commit, slow things in CI. E2E in a pre-commit hook was “so painful… everyone would just turn it off.” If you can’t build a full pipeline, run some tests in one. [L81]
Know how you’ll deploy before building (a theoretical guess); work it out concretely during the walking skeleton. [L25]
Testing ROI pays off here. Manual walkthroughs are fine at first but slow everything down as features grow. [L25] [L93]
Anti-patterns
Relying on manual testing/walkthroughs as feature count grows. [L25]
Only running front-end component unit tests instead of feature-level tests. [L25]

### The Theoretical Guess Points: Problem → Solution → Architecture [L65] [L23] [L85]
Theoretical before physical
Design is abstraction: “thinking before doing”, what vs how, theoretical vs physical, upfront vs emergent. So why model a domain in code before you know the problem, its solution, and the architecture you’ll implement it with? “You can’t really do the stateful part unless you made your theoretical guesses first.” Khalil restructured the course around this. [L65] In the value creation system the rule of the game is the same: go left to right, theoretical first, then physical. [L85]
The roadmap, top to bottom [L65]
Problem space — start from the problem and the people, not the code. Crunch domain knowledge with your boss or the domain experts. Carry three things into the solution space: the problem, concrete examples of what you’re building, and concrete examples of why.
Subdomains — break the problem into parts.
Bounded contexts + domain model — the solution space. A domain model is different from an understanding: you decide which abstractions to codify and agree on names. “No, it’s going to be called this. And here’s how it works.”
Drive it through a concrete scenario — “Person is at state A, person wants to be at state B, and here’s their experience.” Event storm that scenario to find the events, state changes, and the systems you’ll build, buy, or reluctantly build (core, generic, supporting).
Decide how to assemble it — try out systems, build vs buy, how the bounded contexts connect (modular monolith, microservices, integrations). Keep it simple.
Then build — down into the physical guess points (the stateful guess point: value objects, entities, identifiers). See the FeedBook case study for the full walk.
“Build tic-tac-toe”
A context-free kata. [L65]
“Web-based multiplayer tic-tac-toe”
Adds concurrency, client and server domains, creating games and rooms, setting up games, network issues, rendering. [L65]
“Tic-tac-toe on a McDonald’s order terminal”
The problem changes again and exposes different subdomains. “Just changing it ever so slightly really changes the work that we have to do.” [L65]
Inputs/outputs bound the work. Treating each guess point as a system constrains the many possible activities and tells you when to move on. [L21] [L23]
Zero theoretical guesses made (new product, entrepreneur)? Maybe don’t run multi-loop TDD at every guess point. Push one theoretical guess through as fast as possible, then “start playing defense” with tests at the physical level. [L85]
Platform strategy: once shared infrastructure exists (deployment, architecture, known services, tested code), later features can skip guesses at some guess points and score faster. [L85]
Order of work comes from the ideal developer workflow and FA²STR, which start by clarifying the theoretical guesses. [L93]
Anti-patterns
Modeling the domain (stateful guess point) before the theoretical guesses are made. [L65]
Code-first mindset: jumping to solve problems with code. [L65]
Working from code toward the problem produces work that “doesn’t matter long-term” and extra complexity. Coding up toward the vision “always results in a mess.” [L21] [L93]
“If nothing else, the most important thing to understand is abstraction. It’s the thinking before doing.”[L65]

How to actualize it [L21]
Building for a customer
Talk to the customer, the person you’re building for. Prototype. Wireframe.
Joining an existing product

Exit criterion for the problem guess point [L21]
You can say who you help, what they achieve, what they can do, and how you help them, and you have good-enough qualitative and quantitative data that it’s worthwhile (“a fair amount of confidence”). Then move to the solution guess point. The problem guess point’s input is phenomena from the world; its output is your idea. It covers idea generation and validating that there’s a problem, and needs no code or DDD. [L85]
The first guess is theoretical: “does anyone even care?” (e.g. an anime debate app). Value identification is how you validate it. [L93]
Value depends on roles and context. DDD Forum was written only to practise DDD: value for Khalil as learner and educator, not for real customers. Reframed for conference organizers, it gains a real reason. [L93] [L82]
DDD without value teaches you only the patterns. You skip the important part: crunching and refining a domain model. “A robust domain can only come from trying to build something of value” (student summary, endorsed). [L93]
Self-check while coding: “what is the actual value of me doing this thing… how can I connect this to a role’s goal?” If you can’t answer, you’re thinking forwards (reacting). [L93] Check against the three roles and goals. [L82]
Asking for design help? Khalil’s standard Discord reply: “Do you have clarity on the theoretical guesses first?” Share examples and user stories, not tool questions. [L21]
Anti-patterns
Writing code without a clear understanding of why. [L21]
“How do I do XYZ with this tool?” before the theoretical guesses are clear. [L21]
Building features with no purpose (“just build these endpoints”). [L82]
“Before we get into the how, before we start talking about code, we want to clarify the who, the why, and the what.”[L21]
### Role → Goal → Features → Concrete Examples → Acceptance Tests [L82] [L65]
A 1-2-3 chain that drives all work. Role = who, goal = why, features/capabilities = what, concrete examples = how, and the examples become acceptance tests. “Never start a project without these four words clearly understood.” Khalil derived role-goal from metaphysics (Aristotle, Sartre’s existentialism) and later found it already existed as impact mapping and feature injection. [L82] Other problem → solution processes: BDD’s feature injection, DDD’s impact mapping, agile’s user story mapping. Role-goal is Khalil’s “mishmash”, more responsibility-driven: concrete people (actors), the roles they play, their goals. [L65] It all “stems from BDD.” [L82]
From example to executable spec
An acceptance test is a concrete example (Given/When/Then) put straight into the codebase to drive work. It is a shared executable specification for frontend and backend. It runs through four layers: concrete example → test runner → test code → protocol driver (acts “as if it is the user talking to the system”) → system. [L82] Details in Testing.
First framed as “he is billed / emailed access”, then shrunk to “places an order”, because billing and emailing should be temporally decoupled (pattern-first). [L82]
Then-clause tense: past tense usually fits (“order placed”, “email sent”). Present tense (“I should now be logged in”) is fine for UI experience. The hard rule: describe the system the way the user interacts with it. [L82]
The highest theoretical abstraction in code is the feature file: stories, acceptance criteria and a concrete-examples table. If someone can read it and see exactly what the software should do, you have the top-level abstraction; everything else goes deeper into implementation. [L93]
Before — API/DB-first: tables and endpoints, but no who, why or example
After — derived from role → goal → feature → example; the test is the “flag post”
A student’s “abstracted APIs on top of the vertical hierarchy” is an API-first approach starting at the code level. Khalil calls it extremely common and recommends BDD in Action. [L93] Code-first also means thinking in endpoints instead of features and testing manually with curl. [L82] Features then map onto vertical slices.
Artifacts for product-minded teams [L82]
Role-goal (≈ impact mapping, feature injection), process modeling, user story mapping, example mapping. Books: BDD in Action, Specification by Example. Advanced stance: skip mandatory document types and just go through the problem, solution and architecture guess points. “The artifacts, of course, they are a side effect of the shared understanding,” and they go stale quickly.
### Domain Discovery [L22] [L93]
Definition
Getting very clear on the language used and making sure everyone else understands it. [L93] Grounded in DDD’s premise: “If we can all be working upon a shared understanding, a shared mental model of the problem of the domain, then we’re going to have a lot more high quality code.” Its goal is the solution guess point: given the who/why/what, what will we build, what will someone experience, and concrete examples of how it works: features, stories, acceptance criteria, examples. [L22]
Why language matters
Words → images → action. “As a developer, you are primarily just working against mental models.” Names get read and form images in other developers’ minds, and that’s how they know what to do. Naming variables, folders, classes and functions has the same effect as folder structure: design = discoverability and understanding. [L22]
Example: a pet clinic’s language revolves around animals, pets, reservations, procedures, medication. [L22]
A solved problem is a domain model. Khalil logs life problems in Notion. Solving one produces systems, knowledge, language, processes and symbols: a domain model that could become a domain model implementation. The Software Essentialist itself is one. [L93]
Stability → domain layer
Make some code super stable so everything else can rely on it, and put the most important knowledge there: the domain layer at the centre of clean/hexagonal/onion/“stereotypical” architecture. Businesses rarely change wholesale (“They’re probably not going to switch to selling hot dogs”), so you can keep refining the domain model and its implementation. [L22]
Boundaries
Every app also has generic domains: users, auth, notifications, email. Side domains can even become the business (Slack came out of the game Glitch, “I believe”). Boundaries keep problems contained and let you decide per area: invest, scale, rip out and restart, or swap vendors (replace Auth0). [L22]
Context mapping
Separate contexts, apply namespacing boundaries, and be clear how the boundaries talk to each other. Gives high-level clarity on how you solve the problem. [L22]
Core / supporting / generic
Modeling a concrete scenario reveals which systems you build yourself (core), build reluctantly because they matter (supporting), or pull off the shelf (generic). [L65]

### Strategic Design [L23] [L93]
Definition
Borrowed from DDD: making very smart decisions about how we piece together our architecture. Architecture = “the large-scale decisions that we’re going to make and we make the majority of those decisions very early on in the project.” It sits at the transition from the solution guess point to the architecture guess point: “how do we realize this in the real world?” [L23] DDD’s version (Vernon’s “red book”) is deciding which components to build, outsource, or build anyway (core/supporting/generic). Khalil’s version adds clarity on subdomains/contexts, the tools, and four abilities. [L93]
Requirements drive architecture
Problems strategic design must answer [L23]: which components to use without getting stuck; what to build yourself vs install (an NPM package) vs use as an external service; how to integrate components that “just aren’t integrating”; what to do when no component exists, or when it isn’t fully related to your core problem.
The 4 things every developer must be able to do [L23] [L93]
1. Stereotype components
Look at any library, framework, tool or platform (an “architectural component”) and quickly identify which aspects, concerns and responsibilities it helps implement, and where it belongs. Many devs struggle here, e.g. “where does React Query go?” Example: Next.js SSR plus libraries realize the SEO sub-aspect of a system.
2. Develop components
Build components yourself when none exist. This leads to responsibility-driven design.
3. Integrate components
Make systems that don’t talk to each other work together by filling the object design gap / responsibility gap with glue code. Example: Discord ↔ course platform, done with little time as a quick HTML form that posts to a hosted service, which calls the Discord API.
4. Prioritize components
Choose which contexts, domains and modules to build first, generally those leading to the most business value. Core / supporting / generic: build, buy off the shelf, or avoid depending on. Should your core depend on Notion’s API, which may change? [L93]
RDD: the deepest level [L23]
“Responsibility-driven design is about seeing software as a bunch of just different responsibilities, which are doings and knowings.” Responsibilities group into roles, roles collaborate, and objects or functions (the “syntactic sugar”) fill and play those roles, like actors in a Broadway play. It answers where things belong, how to organize concerns, and how big an object should be. “This is the deepest level of design I can teach you. Once you learn it, you learn how to really design anything.” Glue code is RDD and object design, Khalil’s “favorite topic right now.” [L93] Depth: Abstraction Levels, OOP & RDD.
Why responsibilities: they are the “atomic, deepest level” for thinking about code. Thinking there lets you stitch anything together. [L23]
Build vs buy: don’t build email or auth yourself. If you’re building something like Google Calendar, scheduling is core, so keep control of it. [L65] Build the core; use off-the-shelf pieces for sub-aspects where possible. [L23]
It’s an art. Strategic design is decision-making, and “it only gets better over time as you make more and more decisions.” [L23]
Where it’s used: best-practice-first, responsibility-first and pattern-first phases. [L23] The architecture guess point then covers tools, tests, CI and whether to go event-based from day one, before the walking skeleton. [L93] More in Architecture & Code Structure and the FeedBook architecture guess point in the DDD case study.
Anti-patterns
Getting stuck choosing components, or not making good decisions. [L23]
The pattern-first plateau: devs who go code-first → best-practice-first → pattern-first often think only in patterns and can’t go deeper to responsibilities to reason strategically. [L23]
Building commodity systems (email, auth) yourself. [L65]
“Strategic design is, as you would imagine, a little bit of an art because it’s about strategy and it’s about decision-making.”[L23]

## FA²STR, Guess Points & The Ideal Developer Workflow
Three tools fit together. FA²STR is the design method you run on any problem. The guess points tell you which level you are testing at. The ideal developer workflow orders the work from theoretical clarity to a deployed feature. The workflow is the same for an MVP and for a mature product; only the effort per step changes. [L27] [L26] [L40]
FA²STR
Find → Architect → Automate → Specify → Test → Refine. An abstraction over the ideal developer workflow that is easier to remember. It trains the main skill, problem decomposition. [L27] ↓
Guess points / test levels
Stateless (1), stateful (2), application (3), outgoing adapter (4), system (5), incoming adapter (6). “It is just all inputs and outputs… It's feature decomposition.” [L81] ↓
Ideal developer workflow
Clarify theoretical guesses → broad-stroke architecture → walking skeleton → feature work with outer-loop and inner-loop tests. Based on the GOOS double loop. [L26] [L87] ↓

It takes you from a non-trivial design challenge to action steps covering decomposition, architecture, design, implementation and testing, on any side of the stack. It integrates the four pillars (design, testing, architecture, strategy) into one method. [L27]

Upfront vs emergent
Upfront = Find, Architect, Automate (theory). Emergent = Specify, Test, Refine (the physical mechanics: red-green-refactor and the three laws of TDD). They are hard to learn together, so the course drilled the emergent part first on FizzBuzz and Palindrome, then added the upfront part with the Password Validator (see Kata Walkthroughs). Why it exists
The main skill is problem decomposition. The ideal developer workflow has many parts and is hard to remember. FA²STR is a more useful level of abstraction to think with. [L27]
It works for unfamiliar problems. Faced with, say, Spotify-style playlist reordering on the backend or auth on the frontend, you can tell yourself: “No, I actually do have the tool to do this. I know how to walk through this properly.” [L27]
A shared vocabulary lets a team or community break problems down the same way. [L27]

### FA²STR at the Stateless Guess Point [L52] [L51] [L86]
The stateless guess point is the first feedback-loop level: data in, data out, maybe some processing. Problems are simple enough to design in your head. The new idea at this level is awareness that the upfront work exists, even when it happens mentally. [L52] [L86]
Responsibilities: doing vs knowing (from Responsibility-Driven Design)
Every responsibility is either a doing (an action, e.g. a User's setName) or a knowing (knowledge implied by a rule, e.g. getName, or “knows passwords without a digit are invalid”). “All of your requirements ever are going to be things that are done or they're going to be things that are known.” [L52]
That's one doing with many knowings, and “this is really going to be the majority of the tests that we're writing.” [L52]
Rules for each upfront step at this level
Find the guess point from the problem statement. The statement hints at the system under test. Later assignments won't tell you where to start, so you find it by elimination and by mapping the statement to a subject under test. The same problem could be tackled at the end-to-end, application or stateful points. [L52]
Know when to go to paper. “The mind can only hold up to, I think like four pieces of information at a time.” Collaborating objects exceed that, for example autosuggestion in a text editor or assigning a skin to a weapon in CS:GO. “Relationships… implies collaborations, which implies that they're going to be more complex and there's more moving parts.” Use paper, a whiteboard or the responsibility-driven design process (see Abstraction & RDD). [L52]
Automate before code. Have “at least a picture of that… before you begin to write your code.” Sometimes it's genuinely unclear, as with cross-system integrations, web scrapers, browser automation and Chrome extensions. In the course the starter project already provides the scripts. [L52]
Build metacognition now. Practise noticing the upfront steps on easy problems, because higher guess points will demand them on paper. [L52]
Two techniques inside Specify: AAA backwards & programming by wishful thinking [L51]
Overwhelm comes from starting where you are and heading toward a vague end. Start at the end instead: write the assert, then the act, then the arrange. While doing so, pretend the API already exists (“What if it was real?”) so you feel out its design in real time. Start with the most complex example, which settles most of the design early. “Vagueness does not compile.” [L51]
Everything you write is an API for someone else: code, project organisation, test scripts. People “find ways to… circumvent” abstractions they don't enjoy using. [L51]
Don't copy utility classes such as Khalil's Result, value object or entity classes. Students who did ended up complaining about the API. Grow your own through emergent design. [L51]
At the stateless level you only get result verification. Higher guess points need state and communication verification, which is how TDD teaches OO design: “So much of design is just structuring out relationships properly.” [L51]
Use the same backwards technique to design fixtures and builders at the stateful level, “treating your code as your paintbrush.” [L86]
Anti-patterns
Jumping straight to code on problems with collaborating objects, skipping upfront design. [L52]
Writing code before knowing how it will be tested. [L52]
Refactoring before you've seen the duplication three times. [L52]
Starting at arrange and fumbling toward a vague goal, only to find at the assert that the design doesn't work. [L51]

### FA²STR at Any Level: n Loops [L86] [L89]
FA²STR is an input → output system, so the same tool works from a pure function up to an architectural problem such as designing a job queue. [L86] The same system-finding works for, say, a Chrome extension: what's its incoming adapter, and what's its system-level test? [L89]
Start anywhere, then drill in. “It doesn't matter where you start. What always tends to happen. Is you need to drill in. With several. Loops.” A student, Lucas, framed it as “n number of loops” rather than an “nth layer loop.” [L86]
Shortcuts are allowed. You don't have to test at every level. Double, triple or quadruple TDD versus one E2E level and “call it a day” depends on “the score of the game we're playing.” [L86]
Stack theoretical guesses. An extendable architecture or platform lets you skip the Architect step for infrastructure, so physical guesses go faster. That's the case for strategic monoliths and well-designed platforms. [L86] [L85]
Level 3 Find is the hard one. See why level 3 is hard. [L89]
On AI, Khalil's view is that it “crunches down and minimizes” some of the state-change and decision-making logic you'd otherwise write. The skill left is knowing the inputs and outputs you plug it into and the goalpost, and it still has to connect to the rest of the process model. [L89]

Why level 3 matters most
It's “the heart, the core” of what makes your app special. It's pure code with fast tests, full control and easy edge cases, and it gives “crystal clear clarity on the way that our system is interacting with libraries, APIs, and also how it's being called.” Khalil calls it “quite literally life-changing.” [L87]
High-value unit tests on core code should give about 80% confidence in the commit phase. That needs a testing strategy and decoupling from infrastructure. [L81]
Applications force purposefulness: working from level 3 or level 5 stops you modelling an infinite world of entities. [L89]
Why level 3 is hard (and so rarely done)

That's why so few people write high-value use-case tests, and why the course teaches inside-out (level 2 first) while the projects go top to bottom. [L89] Testing is hard to learn more broadly because “you can't really get good at testing without getting good at object oriented design and also understanding architecture.” [L87] Guess point 3 was expected to need 3–4 parts to teach. ### The Ideal Developer Workflow [L26] [L87] [L81]
Definition
A way to put the 12 essentials together to move from the theoretical to the physical through the guess points, on any side of the stack. [L26] Khalil pieced it together from the experts: GOOS (Steve Freeman), Continuous Delivery, Clean Architecture, DDD and BDD. [L40] It's a consistent way to work “95% of the time,” frontend or backend. [L87]
Clarify the theoretical guesses (non-negotiable) — work through the abstraction prism: vision, role, goal, capabilities, down to concrete examples. Khalil's standard answer to problem questions on Discord is “first, we need to clarify the theoretical guesses.” [L26]
Broad-stroke architecture — relatively high level, but clarify the patterns, architectural components and the testing strategy: how you'll verify features work and how tests plug into the deployment pipeline. [L26] This step is for developers: shared clarity on patterns, tools, roles and responsibilities. [L81]
Build, test and deploy a walking skeleton — one slice of functionality taken all the way to deployment, even if tiny. [L26] (See Walking Skeleton.)
Feature work with nth looping — outer-loop tests plus inner-loop tests, then fill in the remaining gaps (~20% of the work) before shipping and moving to the next feature. [L26]
Workflow stepFA²STRGuess points touchedOutput
1 · Clarify theoretical guessesFindProblem, solutionStories, acceptance criteria, concrete examples [L27]

FA²STR is the abstraction layer over this workflow. [L27] Steps 1–3 are the upfront part and step 4 is emergent. [L87] The first steps apply to new features on existing projects too: clarify the theory, know the architecture, know how features are verified, and have infrastructure to run high-value tests. [L26]
Outer loop vs inner loop
Outer loop · customer
Acceptance tests that turn user stories into proof that “this is what the customer wants.” [L40] Written in the user's language at the system boundary (level 5). One acceptance test per feature, shared by frontend, backend, mobile and desktop teams (Dave Farley), and never rewritten per environment. Result verification only. [L87] Run it as E2E or as a high-value unit/integration test. [L26]
Inner loop · developer
Things developers need to know about: logic that isn't a feature but could cause problems if untested, e.g. a validation object for a User or JobPosting. [L26] Unit tests plus incoming and outgoing contract tests. You don't need all of them, and choosing is the “strategy” part. [L40] The main inner test is the level-3 use-case test. [L87]
Per feature, step by step [L87]
Failing E2E acceptance test — system boundary (level 5), in the user's language, from the user's perspective. It can mention pages or things that don't exist yet.
Move in one level — write a failing use-case test at the developer boundary (level 3). About 80% of the time is spent here.
Loop over every scenario — a feature is one success case and many failure cases (“five, six, seven, eight, nine”). Mention things that don't exist yet and carve them out, recursing into smaller objects and back up.
The remaining ~20% — make the E2E test pass by filling in the other guess points (outgoing and incoming adapter tests), then hook up the view: components plus component tests.
Connect everything — the feature is done. Deploy, then tidy up with infrastructure tests (DB, service connections: contract/driver tests). [L81]
Outer + inner loop pair for the sync feature
The CalendarSpy and in-memory fakes need contract tests against the real gateways (level 4) to stay honest. See Testing. [L81] [L87]
Heuristics
E2E: the happy path plus one or two sad paths (check that error messages reach the screen). Then about 12–20 high-value tests with concrete examples, parameterized to add data cheaply. [L81]
Push what E2E can't see down to level 3. You can't sensibly assert “I should get an email” at E2E (automate logging into Gmail?). Verify it with an email spy at the application level. [L87]
New abstraction → new tests. “Anytime we mention a new abstraction, we're going to want to write some new tests.” [L26]
If testing a feature means starting React, React is doing the coordinating. Keep features and their specs apart from the UI, libraries and APIs. [L87]
It's an ideal. Legacy code and cut corners are real. Work toward it, and use it to measure how far off you are. [L87] [L26]
Anti-patterns
Khalil's own mistake: writing all the pure application-layer code first, then trying to deploy, and hitting “all these weird CORS errors.” Deploy as early as possible. [L81]
Building for a month, then finding deployment is hard: “a very bad situation to be in.” [L87]
Mixing feature development with architecture setup, e.g. discovering DB issues mid-feature. It's “a lot cognitively.” [L87]
E2E-only or unit-only strategies: “You're always going to fail.” [L87]
Leaving internal logic untested because “it's not a feature.” [L26]
“It is an ideal and ideals are never perfect. But having this as something that we could use to determine and know how far off we are is incredibly powerful.”[L26]
### From Theoretical to Physical [L72] [L89]
After the theoretical guess points (the FeedBook case study, see Theoretical Design & DDD), you already know how to organise code, you're aware of the stereotypical architecture, and you're committed to vertical slices. “When we move to the physical, we have literally all of this stuff.” [L72] The crossover from what to how is the acceptance test. [L81]
Ideal kickoff on a real project
Get the deployment pipeline working. [L72]
Write the end-to-end test — “Make sure that end-to-end test works in a prod-like environment.” [L72]

In practice, with a familiar standard architecture, Khalil plans, clarifies the inputs and outputs, process-models, then goes straight to level 3 and uses the required inputs to create the needed entities and value objects. [L89]
The course's learning order (inside-out)
Level 2, stateful first — “Because we're still learning the physical, we're actually going to jump to level two.” [L72]
Then level 3 — do the modules in sequence (theoretical → level 2 → level 3), because level 3 has too many responsibilities to find at once. [L89]
Projects go top to bottom — the backend and frontend projects apply the workflow outside-in for real. [L89]
Starting level 3 from one feature [L89]
Create a use case and go through all its success and failure scenarios, letting the needed concepts emerge. Forget the word “aggregate” at first. Ask what goes in, what must come out, and what must change all at once. Don't create base classes such as AggregateRoot until you absolutely need them.
Khalil considered a faster path (skip entities, build success/failure cases, then discover you need entities) because jumping straight to a feature is more motivating, but kept the current order for now. [L89]
### Shipping an MVP & When to Go Light [L40] [L85]
Asked by a student (Aaron) who already had designs ready how to ship an MVP fast, Khalil's answer was to use the same workflow. “Those steps are going to be the same, regardless of if you're shipping an MVP or you're going to be developing a new feature on an existing product.” [L40]
What changes between an MVP and a mature product [L40]
How much time you have.
How much effort you're willing to put in.
The quality of the guesses you've made.
The MVP path: theoretical → testing strategy → physical [L40]
Step 0: learn the mental models — metaphysics and at least the 12 essentials. They work “like landmines and the opposite of landmines,” telling you what to avoid and what to move toward.
Problem guess point — do you have clarity on the problem?
Solution guess point — how you'll solve it and the boundaries between domains. Process-model the one or two user stories you most want to build first.
Architecture guess point — decide the patterns, tools and architectural components.
Testing strategy (the bridge) — outer-loop acceptance tests from user stories, plus inner-loop developer tests (unit, incoming/outgoing contract).
Walking skeleton — draw the architecture, build one user story as a vertical slice from UI to DB, decide the project structure, and deploy it. Expect it to be messy and slow. It's worth it because it sets up the initial testing strategy for the whole product.
Recommended MVP tests — start with either E2E tests or a high-value integration test. Either way you need an acceptance test for customer correctness and a set of tests for the internals.
When to go light
Unvalidated MVP · zero theoretical guesses
The priority is getting real answers from customers. Heavy testing and top quality aren't critical, but build “on a good foundation of guesses.” [L40] Multi-loop TDD at every guess point? “Maybe not.” Push one theoretical guess through as fast as possible, then “start playing defense and adding tests at the physical level.” [L85]
New feature, existing product
Run the full workflow. If architecture, testing strategy and patterns already exist, the Architect step shrinks to a checklist. [L27] The walking skeleton and pipeline are already there, so start at the outer-loop test. [L26]
Platform · stacked guesses
With shared infrastructure (deployment, architecture, known services, tested code) you can skip guesses at some guess points and score faster over time. [L85] An extendable architecture lets you skip Architect for infrastructure. [L86]
Familiar standard architecture
Plan, clarify inputs/outputs, process-model, then drop straight into level 3 and skip setting up level-5 infrastructure first. [L89] How many loops to test at “depends on the score of the game.” [L86]

### Testing Roadmap by Phase of Craftship [L31]
Khalil’s own testing started badly. He tested things that didn’t need testing (Sequelize models) and wrote flaky E2E tests. “Testing sucked for me.” His fix was a roadmap that follows the phases of craftship. You need “to build up a little bit in all of them [the pillars] in order to do even one of them.” [L31]
PhaseTesting skill to build
Code-firstBuild basic backend operations. The backend is “raw”: no front-end tools or thought-leadership noise, so you think purely about structure and tests.
Best-practice-first (1)TDD mechanics: red-green-refactor, start from examples, parameterize. Simple input-output tests (stateless).
Best-practice-first (2)Non-flaky E2E tests with four layers of abstraction. Test “the entire system as a function”. Run tests in different environments (composition).
Pattern-firstDecouple core from infrastructure (hexagonal / clean / onion, “all different ways to say the same thing”). High-value unit and integration tests, stubs, mocks, edge cases, state and communication verification, then contract tests.

Sources: [L31], with the pattern-first order (result → communication → contract tests) from [L91].
The test type is the boundary you draw
“All tests fundamentally are input-output tests.” The name follows from which systems you include in the boundary: [L31]
Integration test: application → outgoing infrastructure, all the way to the database and the services it uses.
Unit test: application + domain logic, up to but not including the database. Pure core code.
E2E test: the entire system treated as a function.
“You just want the highest level of confidence to have to do the least amount of work based on where you currently are.”[L31]
### Red-Green-Refactor Commit Discipline [L44]
Commits exist to make your work easy to read for whoever gives you feedback. [L44]
Rules
Only commit on green. Committing on red is like pausing Mario mid-jump over the flames: “not a very safe place to be for you to unpause the game.” Green is standing on a platform. [L44]
Two reasons to commit on green, and the message must say which: new functionality, or a refactoring (“all that we’re doing here is changing the structure of the code”). The wording (refactor/design/improvements) doesn’t matter; the intent must be clear. [L44]
Exception: the Emergent Design section (the first assignments). Commit on every phase (red, green, refactor) so reviewers can check your TDD mechanics. From the stateless section onward, go back to committing on green only. [L44] [L56]
Rule of three: after seeing duplication three times, “you’re probably going to want to refactor. In fact, you should.” [L44]
Flip the switch: after going green, deliberately break the code, watch it go red, then restore it. That proves the test can fail. [L44]
Make commit messages express behavior, not internals: not “return result as true and error as null on valid password”. [L56]
Before — red save points, messages that hide intent
After — every commit is a green platform with a declared intent
More commit reviews (“a story of commits”) are in Kata Walkthroughs. [L60]
### Comments Only Explain “Why” [L48]
“Your code should always express what happens. Comments should express why you’ve chosen to do what you’re doing.”[L48]
If code can’t say what it does, that’s a code problem. “Always make that readability change first. And at a last resort, use comments.” [L48]
The last resort is almost always the why, because code has no way to express rationale. [L48]
This comes up again and again in kata reviews: “delete the comments”. [L60]
Before — comments narrate the “what”
After — names carry the “what”, one comment carries the “why”
### Arrange-Act-Assert Backwards & Programming by Wishful Thinking [L51]
Overwhelm comes from not knowing how you’ll make it work. If you start where you are and head toward a vague end, there are endless paths. If you start at the end, there are few. “Vagueness does not compile.” [L51]
Assert — write the expected output first, using the most complex example, so the hard design is settled early. [L51]
Act — write the call that would produce it, as if it already existed. [L51]
Arrange — set up the inputs last. [L51]
Programming by wishful thinking
Imagine the code already exists and write it piece by piece, as if real, even though it won’t compile yet. Judge whether you’d enjoy using the API, then implement it. It is abstraction applied: specify what, not how. It’s the same idea as “tell, don’t ask” and declarative code. [L51] [L81] “What if it was real? Think about that.” [L51]
Before — forwards: arrange first, discover the API is awkward only at the assert
After — backwards: the assert designs the API (a result plus all errors)
Khalil first wrote a single error key. After rereading the spec (“for all errors in occurrence”) he changed it to an array, before any implementation existed. [L51] In the emergent-design demo he also renamed result.result to output.result because it read badly. [L55]
Everything is an API
Code, project layout, test scripts. “Every single time that you’re writing code… is you designing an API for someone else to use.” People will get around abstractions they don’t enjoy using. [L51]
Clarify before you verify
Get clear on the input/output shapes (object vs string vs array) and how each responsibility will be asserted, before you code. [L58]
Don’t copy utility classes
Students copied Khalil’s Result class and then complained about its API. “Don’t copy mine, just do your own”: grow a minimal one through emergent design. [L51]
It scales up
The same move finds state APIs (game.getCurrentPlayer(), game.getGameState()) and builder APIs. At higher guess points it forces you to understand collaborations: “so much of design is just structuring out relationships properly.” [L84] [L78] [L51]
### Naming Tests as Responsibility Specifications [L56] [L59]
A test file has two parts. Responsibility specifications (the describe/it titles) name a doing or a knowing and stay abstract. Examples are as concrete as possible. Drive the code with concrete examples and end up with abstract responsibility specs. [L59] Responsibilities come from Responsibility-Driven Design: everything a system does is either knowing or doing. [L56]
Before — names describe the how (keys, flags, null)
After — names state the what; the how lives in the body
Heuristics from the kata reviews
Lead each new responsibility with one concrete example, and refactor to it.each once you see duplication three times. [L56] [L57] Refactoring applies to tests too: three “Fizz” tests become one “returns Fizz for multiples of three”. [L45]
Encapsulate what varies. Hard-coded reasons in names and assertions produced “too long” tests that still said “too short”. Put the reason in the example data. [L56]
Keep each example’s input and expected output together in one object. [L59]
Parameterized tables keep a suite open-closed: a new example is just a new row. [L61] They’re cheap: parameterize to add “20 more units of data”, and they run fast because they’re pure. [L81]
Don’t over-refine examples, but “never sacrifice readability”. [L59]
Full reviews: Kata Walkthroughs.

Passed in or returned, like the palindrome string. [L81] [L87]

An email-provider sandbox doesn’t validate delivery. At most it saves money per run. [L87]
Real network calls to external services in tests slow them down. [L53]

### Acceptance Tests: the 4-Layer Architecture [L82] [L81]
An acceptance test is a concrete example (Given/When/Then) put straight into the code base. It’s a shared executable specification that can drive either the frontend or the backend. [L82] It’s where work crosses over from what to how. [L81] E2E tests “are incredible when they are designed with the appropriate levels of abstraction”. [L31]
Rules
“Then” tense: past tense usually fits (“order placed”, “email sent”). Present tense (“I should now be logged in”) is fine for UI experience. The hard rule is to describe the system the way the user interacts with it. [L82]
How many: the happy path plus one or two sad paths (error messages show up on screen). Then about 12–20 high-value tests with concrete examples and parameterization. [L81] Roughly 10–12 application-level scenarios per acceptance test. [L82]
Idempotent: tests can run “over and over” and still work. [L82]
Existing code: start with a characterization test that pins current I/O end to end (tailor’s pins), then “move in a layer”. Don’t rewrite from scratch. [L81] [L84]
Reusable page objects pay off: a friend’s team using four-tier E2E went “from requirement to implement it with just mixing together a couple of page objects”. [L92]
Three test categories (acceptance, unit, infrastructure): Subject/System Verification. Where acceptance tests run in the pipeline: Deployment & Delivery. Where they sit in the double loop: Ideal Developer Workflow.
### Test Doubles & Hand-Written Spies [L73]
Test double
“A test double is just anything that stands in for the real one.” Skip the spy/stub/mock taxonomy and call them fakes or test doubles. [L73] [L84] “In-memory repo” also sidesteps the naming debate. [L87]
A student used a mocking library to spy on a collaborator and felt something was off. Khalil updated the assignment to require a hand-written spy: “I think you need to learn that. And we all need to learn that.” The fake plays two roles: it implements the email service contract, and it records calls for assertions. [L73]
Before — library-mock soup, inline and duplicated per test file
After — a hand-written fake that implements the contract and records calls
Rules
Give the real collaborator an interface so the fake can implement it. [L73]
Put hand-written spies in their own files, not inline in tests (inline leads to duplication). Hand-written fakes are preferred because growing the contract forces you to implement it. Library mocks are fine only if they “yell at you” when the contract grows. [L87]
Library mocks are for trivial stubs whose behavior doesn’t matter (“this thing needs a logger… here, just take this”). Stateful dependencies are indirect inputs, so use fakes covered by contract tests. [L81]
Wait for the rule of three before extracting a generic spy contract. [L73]
Use controllable fakes to set up “the state of the world” for each edge case: user exists, banned, email taken, username taken. [L87]
### Contract Tests Keep Fakes Honest (and the Clock role) [L81] [L87] [L89]
A common complaint about high-value tests is “we’re not testing anything, just what we put into the tests.” That feeling comes from missing contracts: the fake and the production implementation have no test parity. [L81] Without contracts, mocks create “two divergent code paths”. [L91] The fix is not “stop mocking”. It’s one contract test run against every implementation of the role. [L87]
Worked example: decoupling from time [L89]
new Date() and Math.random() are infrastructure because they touch the system clock and the random generator. “It’s not about where it comes from, it’s about what it touches.” [L84] Time is an application concern (level 3), not a domain (level 2) concern. State machines protect their data and change when told to. Knowing when to tell them belongs to the application, and time can live in its own context folder. [L89]
Before — hidden dependency on the system clock
After — a Clock role with a real and a fake actor
The repository version of the same idea (userRepo.ispec.ts looping over InMemoryUserRepo and the production repo) is in Subject/System Verification. [L81] It’s expensive because it hits the real backend, but it keeps fake and real in parity. [L87]
When the fake is hard to write [L81]
Complex filters/aggregations: find the concrete use cases you actually need, and pull heavy filtering into its own abstraction (e.g. “search”) tested on its own.
Don’t build a do-everything fake filter. Extend the in-memory implementation until the contracts are covered.
If the DB does the heavy lifting, test the contract and “let the external tool do the external tooling”. “Raise the level of abstraction of what it is you’re looking at to test.”
“It’s okay to mock… but just make sure you mock correctly… You want to create parity between what’s real and fake.”[L89]
“What you want is your production code and your test code to be the exact same.”[L91]
### Fixtures = Initial State, Encapsulated with Builders [L78]
Fixture
“What is a fixture really? … It means the initial state.” If arrange/act lines only get the system into a state, they are setup, so move them into a builder. [L78]
Khalil’s example is chess: detect a checkmate (Scholar’s mate, Fool’s mate). It sits at the stateful guess point because it depends on a sequence of state changes. The assertions were carved out by wishful thinking: getGameState() is end and the reason type is checkmate. Ideally the test would “describe the state of the universe” at game end. [L78]
Before — the test is mostly moves
After — a named initial state; the test shows only intent
Rules
Name well-known states in team or domain vocabulary (“Scholar’s mate”, “the most brutal game ever”). Moves could even live in a file. [L78]
Design the builder API by wishful thinking. Treat code as a paintbrush: “we’ll figure out how to make it happen later.” [L78]
Turn in-memory repos into builders (e.g. seed N random users) so tests stay domain-driven and readable. Store plain objects, with a mapper if needed, to keep parity with the real store. [L87]
Real-database strategies need builders even more, because setup is complex. [L64]
Stateful modelling and invariants: Theoretical Design & DDD.
### Value-First Testing Strategies [L64] [L92]
Prompted by a post on “TDD-induced design damage”, Khalil objects to prescribing “one testing strategy 1000% in every scenario”. Learn every way to test, then choose by ROI: time, cost and the quality you need. “There is no perfect testing strategy. It’s going to be entirely context dependent.” [L64]
OptionBenefitsDrawbacksChoose when
A · App-level + real DBTests the DB for real, no mocksComplex setup (builders). Slow at hundreds of edge cases. Alone, it leaves the request → use case translation unverifiedSqueezing “just a bit more” confidence tactically. Add incoming tests.
B · Controller → real DBAs A, plus covers validation and extraction of requestsAs A; “can get very expensive”“A really nice middle ground”, e.g. if you don’t want E2E. Khalil likes it a lot.

Two valid strategies [L92]
Strategy 1
E2E (whole thing, touches the DB) + carve inward with high-value unit tests (no DB). For most task-based UI apps, E2E is the best way to begin and gives the greatest certainty. [L92]
Strategy 2
Incoming (contract) tests + high-value integration tests. Works for any app with an interface. More relevant with many clients (REST + GraphQL). [L92] [L64]
Choosing
“It’s just your level of confidence in your code base.” Logic-heavy apps: many application/domain examples. Simple apps: check that major components (Stripe, etc.) work together. Mixing is fine: “You don’t have to have a perfect parity.” [L92]
This verifies everything before the app call: routing, extraction, validation. It minimizes the uncertainty of Express, “a whole… framework”. A high-value integration test uses the same entry point with the real app, all the way to the DB. [L92] For the application interface itself, see Composition.
“E2E tests, they suck, right?… But there’s very little that’s going to be as good as E2E tests.”[L92]
### “We Don’t Have Time to Test” [L91]
A student’s managers said there was no time for tests. Khalil’s answer was about strategy and roles, not craftsmanship. [L91]
It’s a strategy decision
“I don’t have time” is a limiting belief. It’s really a value choice: fast / good / cheap, pick two. Shipping without tests trades quality for time. [L91]
Capability ≠ choice
“There’s a difference between not being able to do something and not doing something.” All-manual testing scores 1 on subject/system verification, and that is acceptable sometimes. [L91]
Know your role
“Customers [are] supposed to make business decisions and developers make development decisions.” Your professional responsibility is to tell stakeholders the consequences, ideally ahead of time. [L91] Automated testing is an investment, and paying a firm for manual testing is valid if the customer knows the alternatives. [L92]
Practice off the clock
Tire-change analogy: the customer cares about the outcome and wouldn’t want experiments. Practice new techniques on your own time and bring proven skills to work. [L91]
Stop the bleeding — first ask: “do the features that need to work right now work?” Wrap the whole thing in E2E tests (tailor’s pins) before any pattern-first surgery. [L92]
Short on time — E2E tests on the main things, then high-value unit tests if there’s time. [L91] With zero customers, “maybe I’ll just chuck some end-to-end tests”. [L64]
Weird third-party API (e.g. Stripe) — add more outgoing tests there. That’s what “it depends” depends on. [L91]
Switch to test-first — when the potential negative value outweighs the cost of building a little slower. [L84]
Roll it in — apply the approach on the next feature, or on an existing feature in a new branch, and make it part of your infrastructure. [L91]
Don’t
Test only for craftsmanship’s sake. Do what keeps customers and users in benefit. [L91]
Experiment with unfamiliar techniques on paid client work. [L91]

Rules of the stateless guess point (as enforced in the reviews)
Data in, data out. The SUT is a function or a static method on a stateless class. Don’t instantiate it. [L52] [L54] [L56]

### Password Validator [L54] [L55] [L56] [L57]
Problem: a function or stateless class that validates passwords. They must be 5–15 characters, contain at least one digit, and contain at least one uppercase letter. It returns an object with a boolean result and an errors key listing every error that occurred. [L54] Trains: FA²STR (upfront + emergent), AAA backwards, program by wishful thinking. [L56]
Upfront design: Find → Architect → Automate [L54] [L52]
Find
Read the problem. Write success and failure examples for each criterion in a “find” file. Classify the requirements: one doing (validate password) and many knowings (5–15 is valid, no digit is invalid, …). The knowings become most of the tests. Sketch the output for an input that breaks every rule ("s"). [L54] [L52]
Architect
Get clear on the system under test: string in, object out, through a stateless class with a static method or a plain function. At the stateless level this step is “extremely simple.” [L54]
Automate
Set up the test infrastructure. Here that’s just Jest. All of this can happen “in a millisecond” in your head. Writing it down makes the metacognition visible. [L54]
Emergent design: Specify → Test → Refine (commit on green only) [L55]
Valid password — written backwards (assert → act → arrange). result.result read badly, so the variable became output. Fake it: return { result: true, errors: [] }. Commit.
Invalid length — expect exactly one error. errors became its own array, with an “is between 5 and 15” check that pushes InvalidLength. Commit.
Another length example — passes immediately. Commit.
Refine — length examples go into an it.each table (input, expected result, expected errors) nested under a describe naming the behavior. Commit.
Digit feature — the success example passes immediately. The failure example drives hasDigits. A copy-pasted test still expected a truthy result and was fixed. The next example passes. The feature is refined into it.each the same way.
Detect multiple errors — a feature only implied by the statement (“read between the lines”). The expected errors were deliberately listed in a different order from the push order. toStrictEqual failed, and the fix was per-error toContain.
Stopped before the uppercase feature (“pretty much just typing”).
Levels of abstraction in a test file
Specification (feature/responsibility name) → concrete examples → test code → production code. “You always have a higher level of policy that's describing what is happening.” [L55]
Review feedback → fixes
Before — instantiating a stateless class; the test name describes how (keys, flags, null) [L56]
After — static call; the name states a responsibility (“can … / knows that …”), the how stays in the body
Before — hard-coded reason copied across tests: the “too long” tests still said “too short” [L56]
After — encapsulate what varies: password + reason in one row, substituted into the name and the assertion
Before — order-dependent assertion couples the test to push order in production [L55]
After — assert containment: “I don't really care about the order”
Review #2 (Tim) [L57] was mostly praise. It used a plain function (the closest to essential complexity), a fake-it first commit, tiny transformations and toContain (“a very good API”). It also strictly typed the response, which turns the return shape into a role:
Breadth triangulation
One concrete example per responsibility, then move to the next feature. Tim did this. Adding the uppercase test forced him to update older inputs so they kept passing. [L57]
Depth triangulation
One feature, with more and more examples to strengthen its abstraction. Both styles are valid. [L57]
Next step for Tim
Near-duplicate standalone tests → it.each rows of input + expected errors, then many more combinations. Name multi-error tests precisely: “missing digit and missing uppercase letter.” [L57]
“You want to decouple the how from the what. That's huge. Because the how could change. But you still want the what to stay exactly the same.”[L56]

Clarify before you verify (new Find-step principle)
List the responsibilities from the description.
List concrete examples.
Decide the output data structure (object / string / array) and strictly type it.
Decide how you’ll verify each responsibility.
The reviewed student did this in the first commit: calculateStats(numbers) returns a typed stats shape. The commits then showed baby steps. [L58]

Responsibility specification vs examples

Refinement budget

### Military Time Validator [L60] [L61]
Problem: given a string that may be a valid or invalid military time range (e.g. "01:12 - 14:32", format inferred), write a function or stateless class that detects which. Trains: picking the simplest transformation with the Transformation Priority Premise, here and in the Boolean Calculator. [L60]
Transformation Priority Premise (simplest first) [L60]
nil — return nothing. Fake it can start here so the first test fails for the right reason.
constant — return a hard-coded value.
combine constants — e.g. add two constants.
scalars / arguments — use the input.
single statement — one functional expression calling functions or “service providers” such as min/max.
conditional + return — multiple statements.
more conditionals, more statements — variables and mutation sit here, at the costly end.
Using TPP
Use it at FA²STR’s Test step (keep the obvious implementation at the simplest option every time) and at the Refine step. [L60]
“If you feel like you need to add a variable or you think you need to do some mutations, question your transformation.” [L60]
For reference, a condition-map abstraction on test one sits around “level seven or eight.” [L63]
Review #1 (Loki): functional decomposition, commit by commit [L60]
“Some of the most readable commits in a story of commits” Khalil had seen. The problem was decomposed down to the tiniest responsibility and composed upward:

After — tiny responsibilities composed upward; helpers encapsulated by not exporting them
Before — “revise index file for clarification” added comments that restate the names [L60]

Review #2 (Kareel): class-based, “an exemplary demonstration” [L61]

Levels of abstraction, highest → lowest
You explaining the problem out loud to someone.
The specifications.
The test code.
The first level of production code: the function or static method that kicks off the SUT, reading as the algorithm’s steps.

### Boolean Calculator (Design It Twice) [L62] [L63]
Problem: evaluate a boolean expression given as a string, such as "TRUE", "FALSE", NOT, "TRUE AND FALSE", and later parentheses. It has “one other aspect” that makes it harder than Military Time. Khalil flagged it as a kata that may need upfront design. [L62] [L63] [L60] [L54]
Johan: emergent, swim lanes [L62]
“One of the best ones I've seen so far.” Tests are grouped into describe swim lanes (incorrect input, TRUE/FALSE, NOT, AND), which is categorical thinking, so it’s obvious where the next test goes and you can triangulate within a lane. Transformations are simple. Trouble starts at AND: two tests in one loop, deeply nested describes, and vague words (“parts”, “one/two/three”).
Shannon: upfront, one big guess [L63]
All tests pass, but the first commit already had describe.each table infrastructure plus a “condition map”, a stack, tokens and operands. That’s an upfront design decision (FA²STR’s Architect), “five, six, seven different guesses all at the same time.” The design lives in the author’s head, not in commits a reviewer can follow.
Johan’s sequence [L62]
Invalid input → throw — obvious implementation, “a great first defensive coding commit.” Rename the context to “when input is not correct.”
TRUE → return true — the simplest thing, even with foresight. “Lead with the guess.”
FALSE — evaluate the condition inline instead of adding an if.
NOT — “input includes NOT”. Similar code appeared twice, so no refactor yet (rule of three).
AND — two tests at once (flagged). Split on AND and require every token to be true. A refactor introduced transformValuesToExpression, and words like values and expressions began to appear.
TRUE AND NOT TRUE — checkNegation works, but the test name was hard to read.
Before — conditions encoded as nested describes: high “psychic entropy” for the reader [L62]
After — lead with the concrete example. “It's always going to be easier to just use an example.”
Before — an if adds a statement and a branch (lower on the TPP list)
After — evaluate the condition inline: one expression, the simpler transformation
Before — test one arrives with table infrastructure and a condition map: TPP “level seven or eight” [L63]
After — “take one example, write it as English, make that your it statement”; production starts at return true
Design it twice
Once you have a working upfront solution, redo it and arrive at the same place emergently, one guess at a time (from A Philosophy of Software Design). “Allow the tool to be doing the heavy lifting, which is to make the small guesses and lead with that.” For stateless problems, start with plain it tests and refactor toward describe.each/it.each later. [L63]
Name the concepts (ubiquitous language)
“What is a part really?” A part, a token, a value, an expression? Name the concepts instead of numbering them, and refactor test names for meaning too. Pushing complexity into deeper layers is fine: “That complexity needs to go somewhere and it's essential.” [L62]
Both reviews end positively. Shannon’s code “looks really elegant”, and nested statements get refined in the later stateful sections, not here. It’s a spectrum: if you’re confident and short on time, the whole thing can be an obvious implementation. [L63]
“All of what is happening here is happening within your brain. It's not happening within the code to be read as a story.”[L63]

### Roadmap: The Theoretical Guess Points [L65] [L88]
Design = abstraction
“If nothing else, the most important thing to understand is abstraction. It's the thinking before doing.” What vs how, declarative vs imperative, theoretical vs physical, upfront vs emergent are all the same idea. It follows that theoretical guesses come before physical ones. Why model a domain before you know the problem, its solution and the architecture that will implement it? [L65]
Start from the problem and the people, not the code. Crunch domain knowledge with your boss or the domain experts. [L65]
Carry three things into the solution space: the problem, concrete examples of what you’re building, and concrete examples of why. Several techniques get you there: BDD’s feature injection, impact mapping, user story mapping, and Khalil’s role-goal “mishmash”. [L65]
Drive everything through a concrete scenario: “Person is at state A, person wants to be at state B, and here's their experience.” Event storming without one leaves your brain “like mush”. [L65]
Framing changes the work. “Build tic-tac-toe” vs “web-based multiplayer tic-tac-toe” adds concurrency, client and server domains, rooms and network issues. On a McDonald’s order terminal it changes again. [L65]
Order, via the value creation system: problem → solution space (domains; core/supporting/generic) → architecture (build vs buy, tools per subdomain) → physical guess points via the ideal developer workflow. “You never want to be making physical guesses unless you have your theoretical guesses sorted.” [L88]
The hard part is the what. “What makes programming really hard is not the programming part.” Anxiety at work signals a lack of clarity. [L89]
Assignment sequence

### Bounded Contexts, Cohesion & “Be Aristotle” [L67] [L68]
Bounded context
A major domain formed by combining subdomains that use the same language. [L67] Khalil’s extension: “placing a boundary around the context of language that is allowed to be used within a sphere of the domain of that problem or knowledge space.” A chat definition he liked: subdomains are what your app needs, and bounded contexts categorize them. [L68]
Skip subdomains? Fine if you’re experienced. Khalil usually goes straight to bounded contexts. [L67]
“Act like you're Aristotle”: for each item, ask “is this actually part of that topic?”, then combine and name. Example: book feedback + emoji management share language → Feedback. [L67]
It’s all language. “This is all language. So it's language and it's crunching language.” That’s why it’s hard, and hardest for people who struggle with English or creative thinking. [L68]
Add rather than take away while grouping (e.g. add “Google integration” next to Notion). New language emerges. In Feedback, reactions, comments on lines and comments on whole chapters appeared, with the verbs posting a comment and posting a reaction. [L68]
Coupling & cohesion at every level
Grouping subdomains is “coupling and cohesion at the problem level. We're doing coupling and cohesion everywhere.” Khalil files coupling, cohesion, RDD and category theory under one umbrella he calls abstraction design: “what belongs together. What doesn't belong together?” [L67]
Cohesion
How well things belong together. Given JobScheduler and BurgerCreator, lettuce goes with BurgerCreator. [L67]
Coupling
Referring to something you aren’t related to. “It's just really about a relatedness and it's about tightness to what it relates to.” Khalil admits his example isn’t the best. [L67]
SRP at two levels
Problem level: “single bounded context, with clear responsibilities.” Class level: a class whose responsibilities are cohesive. [L68]
“You're placing a boundary around the context of language that is allowed to be used within a sphere of the domain.”[L68]

### Architecture Guess Point: Modular Monolith & Event Bus [L71]
This is the last theoretical step. “Once we get to this architect step, we've done the majority of the hard work.” The modules are already known from the bounded contexts. [L71]
Questions Khalil asks
External systems, components, modules and deployable units (modular monolith vs microservices). [L71]
Components such as Redis, which can play many roles: caching, Pub/Sub, key-value storage, and queuing/scheduling with BullMQ. These come up again at the outgoing adapter guess point. [L71]
How do we organize our code? Classification decides it: core = build, generic = import a library, supporting = build reluctantly. [L71]
Non-functional requirements. The three-step RDD process can find the right architectural components (mentioned only). [L71]
Modular monolith ≠ single process
“Modular monolith does not mean modular process. This is just how you organize your code.” At bootstrap you choose which modules share a process. For FeedBook: billing is external, the core runs together, and sessions/scheduling likely run in a separate process. “Just usually a better place to start than microservices.” [L71]
Event bus
A required component that lets modules talk: everyone registers events and subscribes. Start with everything in one process. Students suggested SOA without a bus, or WebSockets. The course goes event-driven. “At the end of the day, we just need coordinators.” [L71]
Encapsulation: FP vs OOP
Encapsulation means data and related behavior together, with the data protected. FP does it by co-location. OOP does it with a private constructor, a static factory, and restricted getters/setters. “The technique is encapsulation, but the way you implement it is a little bit different.” [L71]
Next, moving to the physical: the ideal workflow is deployment pipeline → E2E test in a production-like environment → level-3 tests. For learning, the course jumps to level two, the stateful guess point, to “encapsulate the hell out of the object correctly” with value objects, entities, domain events, Result and Guard. ### State Machines, Invariants, Transitions & Events [L74] [L76] [L86]
Stateless FA²STR work (feedback loop level one) only transforms data: “mom” in, a result out. “In the real world, we're always dealing with statefulness. Problems are stateful.” You solve them by introducing state, which then has to change correctly, at the right time, and never at the wrong time. [L74] [L86]
State
A position the system can be in. Being at a state is itself data. [L74]
Transition
A move from one state to the next. It “feels functional because it is functional”, so the paradigm doesn’t matter. [L74] [L86]
Event
Created on every transition: data saying “we just moved to this state”. States and events are two sides of the same coin. [L74]
Invariant
“Invariance equals a rule, which really equals business logic.” It blocks invalid transitions. [L74] Also: which states your concepts may be in, e.g. a washing machine can’t be “off” with settings selected. [L89]
Encapsulation’s purpose
“To protect the way the model changes.” “You don't want to be able to put the system in an invalid state.” [L74] [L86]
Washing clothes by hand, built from the end
Start at the goal (clothes clean) and work backwards: clean ← dry ← washed ← washing materials assembled ← clothes gathered. “You see what happens when you start from the end? I'm telling you. It's the technique.” [L74] Thinking backwards gets its own lesson. [L75]
This sketch throws. By the implementation demo, expected domain failures are returned as typed errors instead (below). [L77] Real-world invalid states include blowing into a Nintendo cartridge, restarting the router, and an Uber Eats order placed by accident during a phone call. [L74]

Keep time out. A state machine holds and protects data and changes correctly when told to. Knowing when to tell it is an application concern. [L89] The domain model must be pure: no new Date(), Math.random() or setTimeout. Inject a clock. [L88]

### Aggregates: Minimum Data, Transaction Boundaries [L88] [L89]
Minimum data for a command
“An aggregate is the minimum amount of information required for you to perform a command.” It’s the absolute minimum needed to execute the command and evaluate its rules, and the result is saved into an event. It isn’t necessarily the input data. [L88]
Transaction / consistency boundary
“It's a transaction boundary”: the essential data that changes together in one transaction. Aggregates are application-level (feedback loop level 3). Entities and value objects are level 2. “More of a philosophical thing”, and maybe not a real code construct. [L89]
Not a DTO
A DTO is “data in a certain shape” with no behavior, e.g. the input that invokes a command. A student’s definition, “objects that enforce invariants”, got “fair enough”. [L89]
Keep them small
Per-command aggregates. createUser needs an email and password (email unique, password valid). editUser needs id, names and email, but not the password. A new rule (edit only if verified) turns Email into a value object with a status. Otherwise the User aggregate grows with every command until a simple edit loads “9,000 other things”. [L88]
Rule of three: command-specific aggregates may later refactor into a common abstract User. [L88] “I don't even like to give things concrete base classes until I absolutely need them”, so skip an early AggregateRoot. [L89]
Reference by ID. Loading an Author with 30 feedback cycles for every request is bad and may crash things. [L89]
Lists: design for the worst case. Moving a playlist track from position 3 to 50 shouldn’t update every item in between. Use previous/next pointers, and update the rest later (eventual consistency). [L89]
Boundary test: walk the state-change code line by line. If the system could die midway and restart half-done, the boundary is wrong. [L89]
Two state machines → a domain service, e.g. syncing a tasks list with a calendar into a sync plan (adds, updates, removes) that the application layer applies. [L88] [L89]
Three ways to model them: an OO Order class, per-command data slices, or pure functional inputs → outputs. Khalil won’t give “solid statements” on which is right. [L89]

### Levels of Realness: Interfaces, Abstract Classes, Classes, Objects [L79] [L84]
A student built a TrafficLight class and then extracted an interface from it as its “public API”. Khalil used that to explain a spectrum of realness, running from “not real at all” (interfaces) to “completely real” (concretions). Many developers struggle with when to use interfaces, abstract classes and concretions, and with what a contract is. [L79] Khalil calls levels of realness “a really key concept”. He dislikes OOP taught as “here’s a class that does this” and wants each tool traced back to a purpose, which here is writing scalable code. [L91]
Interface / type
“A pure abstract conceptual idea of what it is that something should be.” It describes a role, like a job description that exists before anyone fills the job. A TS type sits at the same level. [L79]
Abstract class
Both concept and real. It has some implementation (e.g. a standardized getById) plus abstract members you must implement. Best example: the React Component, where you implement render. [L79] [L84]
Class
Real, but it needs new. It’s a blueprint, so it is slightly less real than a plain object. [L79]
Plain object
Real right away. TrafficLightProps as a type describing a plain object is fine. It’s a typing shorthand that avoids getter/setter boilerplate. [L79]
Rules
Only make something abstract when multiple different actors can play that role. Otherwise default to a concretion. [L79]
Choose the construct by the concept’s intended level of abstraction, not by habit. [L79]
When you do need a contract, start with the most abstract thing, an interface or type. Move to an abstract class only when the rule of three shows a real need to share implementation. [L84]
Use contracts at infrastructure seams (repositories, and a Clock around new Date()). That is what lets you decouple from infrastructure at the upper guess points. [L79] [L84]
Anti-patterns
Extracting an interface for something only one actor will ever play, such as the traffic light. [L79]
Treating interface extraction as a “public API” declaration with no role behind it. [L79]
Reaching for abstract classes first instead of interfaces. [L84]
“Abstraction is pretty much the root principle of design.”[L79]
### Roles vs Actors, Contracts & the George Clooney Test [L79] [L84]
RDD vocabulary
Role: a job to be played. It stays abstract until an actor plays it.
Actor: the real thing that plays one or many roles.
Responsibility: what a role must do, e.g. “make house out of bricks”.
Collaboration: the interactions between roles (who talks to whom).
Contract: an actor playing a role. implements creates it (“we’re going to play this role now”), there can be several, and extends can create one too.
[L79]
The Broadway play
In Three Little Pigs the roles are the big bad wolf and pigs 1–3. Scenes are collaborations, and “make house out of bricks” is a responsibility. All of it stays abstract until actors play it, and any actor could play any role, even Tina Fey as the big bad wolf. The roles side is declarative (“what should happen”). The actors side is the implementation (“what actually happens”). [L79]
Now write a George Clooney cameo into the play, as himself. Only George Clooney can play that role, so it needs no abstraction. Khalil’s verdict on the student’s traffic light interface: “You George Clooney’d it.” [L79]
George Clooney test
Ask: “Who else is going to play this role?” If the answer is only this one concretion, no contract is needed. With one implementation, contract and concretion “are one and the same.” When a second actor appears (a traffic light on Mars, or a model train traffic light whose lights stay on longer), triangulate the contract. The essentials go into the contract and the details stay in the concretions. [L79]
Before — premature interface: two names, one actor, zero benefit
After — concrete until a second actor shows up
Where a contract earns its keep: the repository role
A use case can be pure core code if it depends on contracts instead of the DB/ORM. In the adapter layer, the UserRepo interface is the adapter: a role. FirebaseUserRepo and SequelizeUserRepo are actors that play it. The use case only cares that someone plays the role. Unit tests pass in an in-memory test double instead. [L84]
A career milestone
Knowing how to deal with contracts: “When I realized how to do this and I got really good at it everything changed.” Pair it with YAGNI and the rule of three. Both fight the urge to add things you “feel you’re going to need”. [L84]
“I can’t get more abstract than a contract and it can’t get more real than an actor.”[L79]
### How Much OOP Do You Need? [L36]
Very little up front. The highest level of abstraction is the high-level steps and events, and implementing them means carrying out their subroutines. The paradigm is an implementation choice under that same abstraction: [L36]
Functional
“Scripts talking to other scripts.” [L36]
Object-oriented
“A network of objects that talk to each other that do work.” Objects hold references to other objects and collaborate. [L36]
Why Khalil prefers objects
Expressiveness, control, and good support for encapsulation and information hiding. The course uses objects from Best Practice First onward and explains them as they come up. [L36]
Learning syntax
Don’t front-load OOP study. A crash course is “probably not necessary.” Ask an AI assistant “what is this?” and “why did we do this?” when you need to. [L36]
“The highest level of abstraction is just the high level steps and events.”[L36]
### Getting Better at Design [L30]
Design = discoverability + understanding
Discoverability is how easily you find what you’re looking for, which is features (the key developer use cases). Understanding is being able to add, change and test them once you find them. Good design means you can do this, and it makes it easier for others. Every developer designs interfaces: “Your code is the interface for someone else to do their work.” [L30]
Structure by the abstraction prism — organize the project the way requirements come to exist, get tested and get implemented (capabilities → features). It’s the most intuitive way to find things. [L30]
Develop metacognition — understand why you understand things. Internal awareness builds external awareness at the same time. [L30]
Establish the patterns — use patterns everyone understands conceptually, and agree on the set at project start. Patterns are mental models with stereotypical responsibilities: a Redux store is an event listener/handler/coordinator type of thing, and React components present the view. [L30]
Study the stages of action — the seven stages of action from Don Norman’s The Design of Everyday Things. [L30]
Empathy check while coding
Will someone else understand what this is, how to use it and why I’m using it, and can they do their task with it? [L30]
Anti-patterns
“Loosey goosey” code whose purpose can’t be understood as one cohesive thing or tied to a known pattern. [L30]
Mixing stereotypical responsibilities, e.g. React components doing more than presenting the view. [L30]
“Getting better at design is really a practice of metacognition and developing awareness.”[L30]
### Responsibility-Driven Design (RDD) [L23] [L38] [L91] [L94]
Definition
RDD sees software as a set of responsibilities (doings and knowings). They are grouped into roles, the roles collaborate, and objects or functions (whatever the “syntactic sugar” is) fill and play those roles. It answers where things belong, how to organize concerns, how big objects should be and how many instance variables they should have. [L23]
A responsibility is the smallest building block: “something that we do or something that we know.” [L94]
List responsibilities — every doing and knowing the feature needs, starting from a concrete example. Don’t worry about order or wording yet. [L91] [L94]
Assign them to roles — identify what the roles are, using object stereotypes and each object’s dominant role. [L91] [L94]
Define collaborations — how the roles work together. “I like to do that, that last part of collaboration last.” [L94]
Find tools — look for existing libraries that implement those roles, e.g. a message queue for scheduling. [L91]
Why go this deep: phases of craftship
Code-first
“React + Firebase, hook it all up, done.” The pieces are big, technology-focused blocks with little design. It gets you a job. [L94]
Pattern-first
The building blocks are services, entities, repos, aggregates and factories. They’re more granular, but you get stuck choosing which blocks to use and how to fit them together. [L94] [L23]
First principles (Responsibility First)
DDD, clean architecture and the rest are “just different lenses on the first principles.” RDD is the way in, and it’s where you design things no pattern, library or framework gives you. Examples: a loading state, a custom router, a unit of work. [L94] [L91]
Four abilities of strategic design
Khalil says every developer needs to be able to: [L23]
Stereotype components: see which responsibilities any library/framework/tool helps implement.
Develop components: build them yourself when needed. This is where RDD comes in.
Integrate components: fill “object design gaps” / “responsibility gaps” between systems that don’t talk to each other.
Prioritize components: build first the contexts and modules that lead to the most business value.
Designing your own abstractions
Abstraction design, meaning building custom abstractions, is “probably the hardest part of software design in general” but “also the most important.” “If you understand the doings and knowings, you could design anything.” Work outside-in: first the conceptual flow (“presenter loads posts” → “show loading” → “presenter has loaded posts”), then the abstraction. [L91]
Before any code
“You should be writing no code unless you’re very clear on who plays what role and why do they need that.” Role → goal → feature → only the essential properties. That’s how you write the minimal amount of code. [L94] For the metaphysics behind it (essence, purpose, form), see Metaphysics.
Where it’s taught: mainly in the Responsibility First phase, which covers the three-step process, bespoke frontend/backend abstractions, object stereotypes, and stereotypical architecture mapped to frontend and backend patterns. It’s practised again in Value First and in the guess point assignments. [L38]
“This is the deepest level of design I can teach you. Once you learn it, you learn how to really design anything.”[L23]

Control center = coordinator + controller
“The key place where all of the main decision making logic happens.” It can be centralized or delegated, and part of it can come from a framework (Express does some routing). React as your control center
Write everything inside React and React is the control center, so you have none of your own and must test through React’s hidden behavior. Push React out to the infrastructure layer and take back ownership of the control center. Then “you can actually test out 90% of the core use cases without ever even starting react.” [L94] More in Frontend Architecture.
One object, many stereotypes
“It’s very rare that an object plays exactly one stereotypical role.” Take a canvas service that’s an interfacer. Add caching and it becomes a structurer too. That’s normal, so don’t agonize over it. [L95]
### “Category Theory” for Design: Cohesion & the Dominant Role [L94] [L95]
When a design feels overwhelming, the building blocks are usually too big and too vague. The student’s “camera service” was “a container for everything related to this type of behavior.” The fix is to descend granularly enough: get all the puzzle pieces (responsibilities) on the table, organize them into categories, then work out how the categories collaborate. [L94] Choosing which stereotype a set of responsibilities belongs to is abstraction design plus categorical thinking, which is coupling and cohesion. [L95]
“We want to lean into the dominant role because this is the category theory here. We’re trying to categorize as hard as possible.”[L94]
Placement heuristics
Find each object’s dominant role. If it plays two roles, consider splitting it into two objects. [L94]
Name responsibilities generally: “allows objects to subscribe to events / notifies subscribers”, not “subscribing to the mouse scrolled event”. [L94]
“Anytime I hear state change or updating something to me, that tells me controller”, or a controller talking to an interfacer/stateful object. [L94]
Controllers delegate the actual change. The controller gets the post, creates the comment and adds it to the post, and the repo (interfacer) saves it. [L94]
Anything that touches peripherals, network, file system or third-party APIs is an interfacer. Rules about how something may change go in information holders or a structurer. [L94]
Keep IO out of the application layer. IO belongs in interfacing ports/adapters. That’s why dependency inversion and the dependency rule exist, and why being pedantic here pays off in testability. [L95]
Watch for objects that take on more responsibilities than they need, and break them out. [L95]
Calling everything a service
Everyone does it, especially in the application and domain layers. Services don’t break down far enough to follow the rules that apply in any architecture (“the laws of software design”), and they lead to references passed around everywhere, like a canvas object spread across many classes. [L94] Code you can’t tie to known patterns is hard for others to pick apart. [L30]
There’s often no single right answer. A controller can be one use case, or it can hold many use cases as methods:
“There is no statically right answer to this... a fun game of coupling and cohesion.”[L95]

Vertical slice vs temporal decoupling. A change inside one use case/transaction (e.g. how the camera changes) is a vertical slice. Updating the canvas projection after ZoomUpdated is a separate feature, which is temporal decoupling. [L95]

## Architecture & Code Structure
An architect makes the smart decisions at the start of a project. Most of those decisions come from knowing one stereotypical architecture (read/write paths, events, layers, the dependency rule) and from picking tools by responsibility and non-functional need. Code is structured feature-first: modules/ for the customer, shared/ for the developer, tests mirroring features. A modular monolith wired by an event bus is the default starting point. [L32] [L92] [L71]
Layer mechanics (dependency inversion, repositories, fakes, the clock contract) live in Horizontal Decoupling. The composition root lives in Composition. This section covers the architecture-level view and code structure.
### What an Architect Does & the Stereotypical Architecture [L32] [L88]
Architect
“What is an architect really? It's about making the decisions at the start of a project that are going to be really smart to make.” The skill comes from experience plus education. Education means knowing the foundational styles and patterns, summarised as the stereotypical architecture, plus a working knowledge of platforms and tools. [L32]
The laws of the stereotypical architecture
The same architecture shows up on frontend, backend and desktop. Hexagonal, onion, ports & adapters and even event-driven are all “one stereotypical architecture.” [L32] [L88]
1 · CQS: read path + write path
“There has to be a read path and a write path.” Commands change the system; queries read it. [L32] Khalil files CQS under vertical slicing. [L91]
2 · Event-based
“We could scale features horizontally because we could just temporally decouple features.” See Temporal Decoupling. [L32]
3 · Layers of concerns
Horizontal decoupling. Put responsibilities in the correct abstractions, assign those abstractions to layers, and let abstractions talk only to the appropriate ones. Layers: domain → application → adapter → infrastructure. [L32] [L88]
4 · Dependency rule
“An abstraction inside of the application layer is not necessarily supposed to know about directly an infrastructure concern. That's called the dependency rule.” [L32]
Domain layer concerns (4): data, rules, relationships between data, processes. The domain model is “anything tasked with representing your data, your rules, your processes and your relationships.” [L88] [L84] Khalil pictures it as “building this little world and just connecting to it using infrastructure.” [L88]
“Once you're aware of the stereotypical architecture and the layers and the concerns within those layers, you can't unsee it.”[L88]
Once you know it, you always know when a concern is missing and where to encapsulate it. [L88] Many devs struggle to piece an architecture together because they don't know what the stereotypical concerns do or where they belong. Khalil's example: where does React Query go? [L93]
Picking tools: it all comes down to responsibilities
A large part of architecture is “choosing the best tool for the non-functional task that you need to accomplish.” You can only choose from tools you know, so keep learning platforms (static sites, SSG, serverless, Docker, Kubernetes, an event bus, MongoDB). ### Structuring a Codebase: Features First [L35] [L29] [L92] [L93]
Judge structure through the abstraction prism, which Khalil calls “the key thing here.” Structure exists to serve the key developer use cases: discover features, work on them, test, extend and remove them. [L35] Khalil revised his earlier pages/components advice. Organise by feature, the level where all three roles collaborate: “I think this is where stuff should begin becoming code… from this level of the feature.” [L92]
North star 1 · Key developer use cases
Discover, understand, test, add, change, execute and remove features. [L29] [L35]
North star 2 · Customers decide
Drive code outside-in from the customer boundary, starting with E2E tests. Building a component library or tiny functions first is “fundamentally flawed.” [L92]
North star 3 · Discoverability & understanding
Open the codebase and see “in a second or two” how to do the use case. This is the hardest part. [L92]
Before — package by infrastructure: features invisible, cohesion accidental
“I hate packaged by infrastructure.” [L88] Two folders for one concept (fake and testing utils) is a cohesion smell. Merge them. [L84]
After — modules for the customer, shared for the developer, tests mirror features

modules/: for the customer

shared/ (shared/core/): for the developer

Two kinds of slice
(1) The module: capability or bounded context, the thick cut. (2) The operation: an API call, a thin input→output cut. Features/workflows sit in between. Cohesion decides how thick to cut. [L93]

Rely on knowledge in the world: src/ and tests/ need no explanation. Call out uncommon folders like packages/ in the README. [L92]

A developer's one main task is to keep delivering user and customer benefit, and “the work that you need to do is entirely on features.” So “if we were to maneuver into the code base, you are looking for features.” Hunting features is the 80/20 of understanding a codebase. [L29]

### Modular Monolith vs Microservices [L84] [L71]
A tech lead planned to rewrite an MVP as microservices with two backend devs. Khalil's framing: that is a theoretical architecture guess made before the problem is understood, and it “has exploded into more guesses for you to make”: observability and telemetry across deployable units, messaging infrastructure, deployment. [L84]
Justify potential negative value
Every architectural complication must be “solidly justified” against the negative value it can introduce. You may not even be aware of the guesses you're forcing on yourself. [L84]
Fewer components → fewer guesses
“The least amount of components you have to manage means the least amount of guesses you have to make.” That's why serverless is popular: you get to focus on the guesses you want to make. [L84]
Extensibility from first principles
Maintainability and extensibility come from vertical slicing plus horizontal decoupling, not from deployment topology. Reading: Vaughn Vernon's Strategic Monoliths and Microservices. [L84]
Modular monolith ≠ single process
“Modular monolith does not mean modular process. This is just how you organize your code.” Several modules live in one project, and at bootstrap you choose which ones share a process. “Usually a better place to start than microservices.” [L71]
How to organise it
Subdomain classification drives folders: core = build it; generic = import a library; supporting = build it anyway, reluctantly. By the architecture step “we've done the majority of the hard work.” [L71] Details in the FeedBook architecture guess point.
External services are collaborators: wrap the minimum integration code (Stripe, PayPal, mail) in a service. [L84] Don't build generic subdomains such as auth or your own SMTP server. [L88]
Event bus as the backbone: everyone registers and subscribes to events. Deploying everything in one process is the easiest start. Students proposed SOA without a bus, or WebSockets; the course goes event-driven. “At the end of the day, we just need coordinators.” [L71]
Microservices in the course: not covered in depth. The course covers the foundations: temporal decoupling, vertical slicing, horizontal decoupling, deployment. [L84]
Anti-pattern: the makePurchase transaction script [L88]
Get cart items → check inventory → bill the card → update inventory → send email, all in one call. A crash between billing and the inventory update leaves an invalid state. Khalil did this on the solidbook site and suffered, with serverless cold starts cutting requests. Instead, model subdomains (e-commerce, orders, billing, emails, inventory), each its own state machine, talking via events.

## Frontend Architecture
The frontend uses the same stereotypically testable architecture as the backend, under different names. Take the control center back from React. Split the app into a read side (gateway → cache → observer → presenter → view model → component) and a write side (component → controller → domain object → repository → gateway). Test the pure middle without starting React. [L33] [L87] [L90] [L92]
### Same Architecture, Different Terms [L33] [L83] [L87]
“The architecture you do on the front end that you do on the back end, you do on desktop, pretty much anywhere. It is exactly the same.”[L87]
“It's all the same stereotypical architecture.”[L83]
Frontend and backend are “the same thing because [architectures] are stereotypical.” Learn the backend patterns first (Pattern First), build with them a few times, then look at the frontend patterns and kinds of state. It will “look a lot like the back-end.” [L33]
Only the terminology changes. Master the architecture on one side and “you've effectively mastered it everywhere.” Khalil planned to build it 4–5 times (backend, frontend, desktop, Chrome extension) to show it's “the same stuff over and over.” [L87]
Framework patterns map onto agnostic patterns. Redux store and single-file components came from the technology. Their responsibilities map onto stack-agnostic patterns: DTOs, controllers, presenters, global state (fundamentally an observer), gateway, view model, data model. All of these derive from the six RDD object stereotypes. [L90]
The frontend has real domains. React Router has its own domain layer (domain/app/adapter/infra) that you install rather than build. “Your app is just a bunch of… domains connected to each other.” [L88]

### React Must Not Run the Show: Own the Control Center [L87] [L94]
Control center
The coordinator + controller objects: “the key place where all of the main decision making logic happens.” It can be centralized or delegated, and there can be several of each (a page may be a controller, page switching may be a coordinator). [L94]
The coordination test
“If you want to test your features, if you need to start react, then react is doing the coordination.” A view library “becomes a framework when you let [it] do the coordinating and controlling.” You're “in really good shape” if you can go from requirement to coded feature without touching or mentioning React. [L87]
Payoff: “You can actually test out 90% of the core use cases without ever even starting react.” Add React “way later” and build the frontend “as if you're writing backend code.” [L94]
How the UI hooks up: components get state from the presenter and tell the controller what to do. That boundary lets you swap React for another view library. [L87]
Wrap framework rules you can't avoid. Hooks force a particular structure, so either design things yourself or wrap “the ugly bits and pieces” behind your own abstractions. [L33] Khalil would extract a routing service rather than use the router hook: “hooks are just the worst… Don't tell me I need to name things with use in front of it.” [L81]
Strategy decides how far to go. An entrepreneur shipping fast can write everything in React, then turn defensive once value is validated. [L94] For small/medium frontends, start with E2E tests and carve out layers only when internal complexity justifies it. “I cannot prescribe to you a single approach.” [L90]
Before: the component fetches, maps, validates and patches the cache, so React is the control center
After: the component only reads a view model and forwards intent
### Read Side: Gateway → Cache → Observer → Presenter → View Model → Component [L90]
Gateway / API client (infra): sends the request (axios, HTTP adapter). [L90]
Cache (infra): stores fetched data. This is global/app state, persisted across screens. [L90]
Observer / coordinator: listens to the cache and notifies subscribers on change. “Global state… fundamentally it is an observer.” [L90]
Presenter: subscribes to changes and rebuilds view models. Its API is something like presenter.load(). It is also an interfacer, getting data ready for the view. Selectors live here. [L87] [L90]
View model: the shape the component actually renders from. Components “shouldn't take on the job of preparing data to be seen. They should just get the data to be seen.” [L87]
Component, below the squiggly boundary (code we don't own). It binds to the presenter via useEffect (or MobX etc.) to re-render: “That's how you connect your pure code to React.” [L90]
Read-side rules
Components must not know about the cache/store (dependency rule). [L87]
Don't put selectors in component files. Selecting belongs in the presenter. [L87]
Reads go straight to the data store and return the simplest shape. Don't load domain objects and reshape them (“mapper hell”). Domain objects are for writes only. [L87]
Get reactivity working in the walking skeleton. [L87]
### Write Side: Component → Controller → Domain Object → Repository → Gateway [L90]
Component: holds ephemeral form state and calls the controller on submit (e.g. the registration page calls controller.register(...)). [L90]
Controller: a cluster of use cases / command handlers, like a backend application service. It can be split per use case (CreateUserController, EditUserController, DeleteTodoController). [L90]
Domain object: the decision maker. “Anytime you intend to perform a state changing command… it must typically pass through a domain object to determine if we should continue with the request or not.” [L90]
Repository: receives valid domain objects and calls the gateway. Repo and gateway may be the same thing. [L90] In DDDForum the frontend UserRepo builds the DTO, sends it, then saves to the cache. [L81]
Gateway → backend. The cache updates and “everything flows back down reactively.” [L87]
The write path is about throughput: controllers and domain objects decide what gets through. Knowing where each piece belongs encapsulates a lot of frontend complexity. It scales to designs like pipes and filters (FL Studio-style effect chains). [L90] Separate read and write code paths so data flows one way. [L87]
Share value objects
Is it OK to share models between frontend and backend? Yes. Value objects and their validation can come from a shared package, so validation isn't duplicated. Frontend-only domain objects like RegistrationForm still exist. [L90] A monorepo with NPM workspaces can share DTOs and even domain objects. [L81]
Frontend domain logic is real
Khalil used to say DDD doesn't belong on the frontend. Now: logic-heavy apps need proper domain models, e.g. a digital audio workstation, a drag-and-drop call-flow editor, or plugin/pedal-board compatibility. Front-loading logic also speeds up feedback because there's no network round trip. [L87] [L88]
Name use cases by intent
“Submit form” is “not a great name.” Prefer “register user”, “edit user”. [L90]
### State Management: Kinds of State & Where Each Lives [L87] [L90] [L94]

Delegated

State management is part of the pattern catalog, with the types of state being global, domain objects, view models and component. [L92]

High-value unit tests on the pure middle. Does creating a to-do add one to be presented? Does creating a user show validation errors? [L90]
Components & adapters
Components are mostly stateless (level-1-style tests). Stateful ones resemble state machines (level 2). Cache hydration is an outgoing adapter test. Incoming-adapter tests matter less on the frontend. [L87]
One acceptance test per feature, shared by every platform
“You only ever have one acceptance test. You don't switch the test for different environments.” Frontend, backend, mobile and desktop teams take the same spec and each validates it in its own way. For example, the frontend may only check a “check your email” page, while email sending is verified at the application level with a spy. [L87]
Picking a strategy [L92]
Strategy A: E2E + carve inward with high-value unit tests. Strategy B: incoming (contract) tests + high-value integration tests. Judge them by confidence, not dogma. Mixing is fine. [L92]
For task-based UI apps, start with a few E2E tests: “there's very little that's going to be as good as E2E tests.” In a messy codebase, stop the bleeding first (the tailor pins before cutting). [L92]
Fakes need contract tests. Run one spec against every implementation (fake and real) so the fake stays in parity. [L87]
Structure for more than E2E. Few people structure frontend code so tests “can go further than just doing end-to-end tests.” [L33]
Page objects. A friend's four-tier E2E setup with reusable page objects made new features “plug and play.” [L92]
### Frontend Anti-Patterns [L87] [L90]
Called out in the course
Queries directly inside components (Apollo-era advice). [L90]
Components that fetch, display and decide how to structure information: “just too much is happening in one place.” [L90]
Using the state-management library for literally everything. [L90]
Selectors or cache access in component files, which breaks the dependency rule. [L87]
Hooks doing coordination: hooks leaking into controllers “mangle your code” and make controllers hard to test. Khalil calls his own DDDForum v2 controller messy because he was “forced to use react hooks.” [L81] [L90]
Letting React be the control center, so you have none of your own and must test through React's hidden behavior. [L87] [L94]
Massive coupling. “Coupling… is the enemy of testing.” Insert a contract between clumps so each can be tested alone. [L90]
Loading domain objects for reads and mapping them into view shapes (“mapper hell”). [L87]
Hoisting toggle state into global state. [L87]
Duplicating validation on frontend and backend instead of sharing value objects. [L90]
Organizing by pages/components or building a component library first (inside-out). [L92]
Only E2E tests because the code isn't structured for anything else. [L33]
Over-architecting small/medium apps without strategic justification. [L90]
“Once you understand this and you understand it very deeply now you understand front end architecture forever.”[L90]
Related: Testing (contract tests, verification types), ideal developer workflow (outside-in, walking skeleton), physical essentials (horizontal decoupling, composition).

## Learning Path, Books & Career
How the course says to learn: climb the phases of craftship in order, learn “topics for problems”, and read a small shelf of books that each feed one essential. It also covers how to stand out as a developer and how to work inside a team. That means knowing your role, being honest about trade-offs, and giving estimates rather than dates. [L28][L34][L80][L91]
### Learning DDD as a Code-First Developer: Don't Jump In Yet [L28]
DDD's toolkit (impact mapping, event storming, process modelling) is held together by systems thinking: problem → solution → architecture → deployment → system → functions. A code-first mind can't “compute” that chain yet. The way in is to climb the phases of craftship, not to read DDD books first. [L28]
Code-first — Build the API/app any way you can. Expect manual curl testing, “endpoints instead of features”, and a DB on your own machine. [L28][L82]
Best-practice-first — Start from an acceptance test. Add E2E on the backend, then the front end, then deploy. Refactor to a monorepo so front and back end can share DTOs and value objects. This is where you get the “tiny hints” of DDD. [L28]
Pattern-first — Learn the patterns in order to carve out a domain layer. Backend: value objects, entities, use cases. Front end: presenters, domain models, controllers, view models. [L28]
(DDD-first →) Responsibility-first — Talk to domain experts before writing code. Then move on to object design and roles/responsibilities. Stopping at DDD-first is “good progress, but we need to move past that”. [L85]
Value-first — Guess points, FA²STR and the ideal developer workflow. These only make sense once you've climbed the earlier phases. [L28][L82]
Why this order
Layers (domain, application…) exist mainly to give you different places to write tests. Learn testing first, or the layers won't make sense. [L28]
Application-layer tests run without React, a DB or a web server. They're “incredibly valuable”. [L28]
Ratio heuristic: about 1 E2E test to 10 high-value unit tests. [L28]
How learning actually sticks
Chunking: your bandwidth is limited, so you need experiences. Redo assignments using a new approach each time. [L28]
You need reference experiences from the tedious katas before abstract ideas have anything to attach to. [L85]
Rewiring from an old paradigm is “freaking painful”. Set milestones and checkpoints so you don't get stuck “in the void”. [L85]
“Topics for problems”
Learn a topic when a problem needs it. Don't spend “five months on domain-driven design” or on railway-oriented programming in isolation. [L91]
Learn the 12 essentials first, then file new jargon under them. CQS goes under vertical slicing, for example. [L91]
A DDD practice project with no real value only teaches you the patterns. You skip the part where you crunch the domain model. [L93]
Khalil's own mistake — “I learned DDD before I even knew how to write tests.” If you jump into patterns without knowing why they exist, you get stuck on the patterns. [L28]
Mastery process (how the 12 essentials were found) — First Observe & evaluate: study the phronimos (practitioners who did it well) and experiment until you reach their conclusions yourself. Then Eliminate what doesn't matter. Then Execute the rest as a “straight line”. [L84][L85]
Deep dive: FeedBook DDD case study · Course map & phases.
### “How to Get Better at X” Roadmaps [L29][L30][L31][L32][L39]
The FAQ answers share one pattern: go through the phases of craftship in order, anchor everything to the abstraction prism, and practice on real problems. Only the learning advice is summarised here. Each card links to the section with the details.
Design [L30]
Design = discoverability + understanding. Can you and others find, add, change and test features? [L30]
Structure projects by the abstraction prism (capabilities → features). [L30]
Use patterns everyone already understands, and agree the pattern set as a team at the start. [L30]
Practice metacognition + empathy: why do I understand this, and would someone else? Study Norman's seven stages of action. [L30]
→ Abstraction, OOP & RDD
Testing [L31]
Learn on the backend first. It's “raw”, with no front-end tooling noise. [L31]
Order: TDD mechanics (examples, parameterisation) → non-flaky E2E with 4 layers of abstraction → decouple core from infra → high-value unit/integration tests and the 3 verification types → choose test strategies. [L31]
Core skill: move responsibilities so you can “isolate exactly what it is you would like to test and forget everything else”. [L31]
Aim for the highest confidence for the least work, given where you are now. [L31]
→ Testing: Principles & Techniques
Architecture [L32]
An architect makes the smart decisions at the start of a project. That takes experience plus education. [L32]
Learn the stereotypical architecture: CQS read/write paths, event-based design, layers of concerns, the dependency rule. [L32]
Try tools on your own time (Docker, Kubernetes, event bus, MongoDB). Know their non-functional reason for existing. [L32]
“It all comes down to responsibilities”: choose the best tool for the non-functional task. [L32]
→ Architecture & Code Structure
Backend [L39]
Pick a problem in your life where you want time back. Build the backend for it and see it through until it actually saves you time. [L39]
Make it public. That “is going to propel you”. The course final project (solo or with a community member) is the place to do this. [L39]
Progression: just build it → acceptance tests + a Docker production-like environment + patterns → roles/responsibilities/collaborations “guided by objects”. [L39]
Do the theoretical work up front, using the abstraction prism. [L39]
→ Physical essentials
Large codebases [L29]
Your work is “entirely on features”, so go into the codebase looking for features. That's the 80/20. [L29]
No acceptance tests? Find the common features by asking people, reading tickets and using the front end. Then trace how each one is realised in code. [L29]
The trace shows you how the original developers thought. [L29]
Target structure: src/modules/<capability>/<feature>, mirrored in tests/ with acceptance tests. [L29]
→ Architecture & Code Structure
Any new field, fast [L82]
Everything is a system: inputs, outputs, environments, processes. [L82]
Isolate inputs and outputs. Start with the role and the goal. [L82]
Go as deep as you can, down to something foundational (money, value). Then climb back up, connecting the pieces. [L82]
→ Metaphysics & Systems
Self-assessment — Treat the essentials as game-style stats you level up. At code-first, horizontal decoupling might be about 2/10, and nobody expects 10/10 during best-practice-first. By value-first, all the physical essentials should be solid. [L82][L91][L93]

### Working in Teams: Know Your Role, Trade-offs, Plans & Estimates [L91][L86]
Know your role [L91]
“Customers [are] supposed to make business decisions and developers make development decisions.” [L91]
You have a professional responsibility to tell stakeholders the consequences of the current way of working, ideally ahead of time. [L91]
If missing tests start to hurt your ability to keep users and customers “in benefit”, say so explicitly. [L91]
“No time to test” [L91]
Fast / good / cheap: shipping without tests trades quality for time. That's a strategy choice, so name it as one. [L91]
“I don't have time” is a limiting belief. It's really a value decision. [L91]
Short on time? Write E2E tests for the main paths, then add high-value unit tests if there's time left. That's what “it depends” depends on. [L91]
“There's a difference between not being able to do something and not doing something.” Don't test only for craftsmanship's sake. [L91]
Practice on your own time [L91]
Tire-change analogy: the customer wants the outcome, not your experiments. Use proven skills on paid work and try new techniques on your own time. [L91]
Roll course material into work on the next feature, or on an existing feature in a new branch. Then make it part of your infrastructure. [L91]
Try unfamiliar tools outside work too. [L32]
Planning is a lie [L86]
“You have plans. You don't have a plan.” Be ready to throw out an approach when reality or users push back. [L86]
Give estimates, not dates. Everything is a guess. Keep personal deadlines from turning into other people's expectations. [L86]
Estimation is a strategy game: look at your current pieces, their state, and where you are. There's no blanket formula. [L86]
Ship a walking skeleton / tracer bullet early. You learn how fast you work and your estimates get better. [L86]
Don't evangelise [L86]
Don't try to get the team to buy into every named technique. Many practitioners already use event storming or impact mapping without naming them. [L86]
Being value-first is about you making better decisions. [L86]
Swap some meetings for async video (Loom), with a grain of salt: “can you give back time and enrich time with meetings?” [L86]
Growing shop, no clear process [L86]
Learn thinking backwards and impact mapping first. [L86]
Build your decision-making: what to focus on, when things get done, how to estimate. [L86]
Stack theoretical guesses (architecture, platform, process) so that physical work speeds up over time. [L86]
